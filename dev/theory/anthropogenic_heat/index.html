Anthropogenic heat from the building energy model

Buildings are the largest source of anthropogenic heat in dense city centres (Sailor 2011). Until issue #29 the urban example prescribed it: a two-peak diurnal profile of 2–40 W m⁻² per surface class, with no source for the numbers. Now the building part of $Q_F$ is what the Phase 50 zone model (Building energy: indoor air and thermal mass) and its HVAC plant (HVAC demand and exhaust heat) actually give to the outdoors, step by step.

1. Where the energy a building uses goes

Everything a zone exchanges with the outdoors appears in one of its balances: the air node and the columns (ZoneBudget), the vapour in the air, the plant (HVACBudget) and the split of the internal gains (InternalGainRates). building_heat_release collects the terms that leave the building into a BuildingHeatRelease, positive outwards:

Summing the zone balance, the vapour balance, the plant's first law and the gain split gives, for every step,

\[Q_{released} + \frac{ΔE}{Δt} + L_v ρV \frac{Δq}{Δt} = P_{electric} + P_{fuel} + Q_{metabolic} + Q_{solar} + Q_{prescribed},\]

where $E$ is zone_heat_content (air and every column) and $Q_{solar}$ the transmitted sunshine. Over a period after which the building is back in its initial state, the heat released equals the energy consumed plus the transmitted sun. That is the premise of the inventory method of estimating $Q_F$ (Sailor 2011). A building model adds the timing: thermal storage delays the release, so a building heated at night gives part of the heat back the next day.

Three consequences hold exactly and are tested in closed form:

  • Steady heating: the flue loss plus the conduction and air-exchange loss $HΔT$ add up to the fuel $Q/η$.
  • Heat-pump heating: the negative exhaust, $-Q(1 - 1/COP)$, taken from the outdoor air, cancels part of the loss, so the city receives $Q/COP$, the electricity.
  • Steady cooling: the air exchange brings $HΔT$ in and the condenser rejects $HΔT(1 + 1/COP)$. The net is $P_{cool}$: an air conditioner adds its power to the city, nothing more, but moves the rest from the canyon's air to the condenser's air.

Direct versus through the facets. A facet element's outer face is closed by the surface energy balance of SurfaceExchange. Heat conducted out of the building warms the skin and leaves as the facet's $H$, $LE$ and emitted long-wave. It is already in the facet fluxes, and adding Q_envelope_facets to the air again would count it twice. So anthropogenic_sensible_heat and anthropogenic_latent_heat are only the direct terms, and anthropogenic_heat_maps maps only those: the exhaust at the building's roof facets (rooftop condensers and towers), the air exchange, glazing and lost gains at its wall facets, into the canyon each wall faces. The map sums to the direct release exactly. The atmosphere above a column receives aggregate_facet_fluxes(u, fl).H .+ anthropogenic_heat_maps(...).H.

2. Zones behind the facets of a street

UrbanFacetScene (#28) gives every roof, wall and street facet its own conduction column and couples their long-wave exchange implicitly. scene_building_zone builds a zone whose envelope is a building's roof and wall facets: the same UrbanFacet objects, wrapped in FacetElements of the facets' areas. Its floor area is the volume over the floor height, and its intermediate floors are internal mass. One zone per building is the TEB-BEM assumption (Bueno et al. 2012). step_buildings! then advances the scene and every zone together.

Each facet column step is affine in two things. One is an extra flux imposed on its outer face; the facets' long-wave exchange acts through it. The other is the temperature of its interior environment; the zone acts through it. Three trial steps per facet (base, +Δq, +1 K) measure the skin increment $x_i = a_i + b_i c_i + d_i y_k$ and the flux into the column at the inner face $F_i = F^0_i + e_i c_i + g_i y_k$. Here $c_i = Σ_{j≠i} G_{ij} x_j$ is the long-wave coupling of #28, and $y_k$ is the change of the air temperature of zone $k$. The zone's own elements (other envelope, internal mass, glazing, air exchange) give its air balance as $\mathcal{L}_k y_k = R_k + Φ_k$ through the #31 affine assembly. The facets add $Σ_{i∈k} A_i (q_i - F_i)$, with $q_i$ the radiative gains on the inner face. Facets and zones form one dense linear system in $(x, y)$.

The air node's time constant, $C_a / Σ hA$, is minutes. The coupling is implicit, so it is stable at canopy time steps. A lagged coupling, where the columns step against the old air temperature and the air against the columns' fluxes, would be unstable there.

Thermostats by active set. A zone whose free-floating temperature leaves its dead band is pinned on the violated setpoint. Its unknown becomes the load $Φ_k$ instead of $y_k$. A load beyond capacity is clamped and the zone freed with that load. A pinned zone that, after a neighbour changed, needs the opposite action floats again. For one zone this is exactly step_zone!(zone, forcing, dt, hvac). With several zones the set settles in a few passes; the loop is bounded.

(2012), Table 3, describe the dense residential centre of Toulouse: 20 m buildings, building density 0.68, wall-to-plan ratio 1.05, 30 % glazing, brick with 3 cm insulation, 0.5 ACH, internal gains 5.8 W m⁻² of floor, set points 19–24 °C, 2/3 electric heating. The validation run builds a 3 × 3 array of 50 m blocks with 10 m streets (density 0.694, wall ratio 1.11). Every block is a zone behind its facets. The run uses the PVGIS typical-year weather for the site (ERA5 temperature, SARAH2 irradiance), with 14 days of spin-up, and averages over 19 Dec–17 Feb. The comparison is the CAPITOUL inventory of building electricity and gas use (Pigeon et al. 2007), as reported in Bueno et al. The test asserts the total within 20 %. Bueno et al. call a 20 % error in building energy use acceptable for urban canopy models. The electricity/gas split depends on how the "2/3" heating ratio and the "electric fraction 0.7" of the gains are read, so it is not asserted. Table 3's wording, "fraction of electric heating systems over gas heating systems: 2/3", can also mean electric : gas = 2 : 3 (40 % electric). With the same run, that reading gives electricity 33.8 (inventory 33.6) and gas 33.1 (inventory 25.0), total 66.8 W m⁻² (+14 %). The simulated plant heats electrically (η = 1): the released heat omits the flue loss of the part costed as gas, 1.4 W m⁻² for the 2/3 reading. The released heat, 69.9 W m⁻², falls inside the 5–100 W m⁻² typical of cities (Chrysoulakis & Grimmond 2016, after Christen & Vogt 2004 and Allen et al. 2011). Dense cores exceed 100 W m⁻² (up to 550 W m⁻² in London, Iamarino et al. 2012).

Changing wall subdivisions from 2 to 1 and spin-up from 14 to 7 days moves the total by 1.4 % (66.65 W m⁻²).

A whole typical year of the same configuration, not run by the test suite, was also computed. It used ideal cooling at 24 °C, which Toulouse's residential centre largely lacks (Bueno et al.'s first hypothesis). It released 51.4 W m⁻² on the annual mean (winter DJF 72.3, summer JJA 46.0), 41.0 of them directly to the air. The largest hourly direct release was 104 W m⁻².

What is not validated. The weather is a typical year, not the 2004–05 campaign. The inventory is city-scale and the model is a regular array. Floor height (3 m), window U (3.1 W m⁻² K⁻¹) and SHGC (0.7), the insulation material, the internal-mass floors (10 cm concrete) and the boiler efficiency (0.9) are assumptions, labelled in validation/support/toulouse_bem_driver.jl. Sailor (2011) itself is paywalled and its tables were not accessible here. The ranges above are quoted from open reviews that cite it.

4. The flagship example

The flagship run (examples/urban/berlin_summer.toml) still runs the slab surface scheme, which has no building interior. Its anthropogenic heat now comes from one city-mean BuildingZone. The zone takes the scene's total volume, footprint and wall area, the DOE medium-office schedules, thermostat and gains (#33), and a packaged DX unit with a gas boiler. It steps every radiation update (10 min) against the canyon air. The release is mapped onto the grid:

  • exhaust (by floors) and the roof envelope at the roof cells;
  • air exchange, glazing, lost gains and the wall envelope at the cells next to the buildings.

The heat goes into the first air layer above each column. The window-transmitted sun is removed from the wall cells' surface budget, which would otherwise absorb it too.

The walls and roof exchange with the canyon air through the ISO 6946 outside coefficient but absorb no sunshine, because the slabs already absorb it. Two other designs were tried in one- and two-hour runs and dropped:

  • Sol-air walls counted the sun twice. The envelope returned −8.7 W m⁻² to the air.
  • Outer face held at the slab skin temperature, with the envelope heat sent to the slabs. That stiff interface, exchanged explicitly every 10 min, swung the slab budget from −64 to +27 W m⁻² (domain mean) between two updates.

Over 06:00–08:00 on 20 June the domain-mean release to the air rises from 27 to 35 W m⁻². The exhaust rises from 4.6 to 18.9 W m⁻² as the offices fill and cooling starts.

Traffic is a separate, constant 8 W m⁻² of urban area over the road cells, the value Bueno et al. use for Toulouse, and is not from the building model.

A bug this replaced. The old code added $Q_F$ to the sensible matrix. That matrix is a loss in the surface budget of step_surfaces!, and the air had already received its sensible heat in apply_surface_flux!. So the prescribed anthropogenic heat never reached the air and instead cooled the surfaces by up to 40 W m⁻².
