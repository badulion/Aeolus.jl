Soil heat and freeze–thaw

Phase 46 (#16) gives the soil column of soil water a temperature. Heat is conducted through layers whose conductivity and heat capacity depend on how much liquid water and ice they hold; water freezes and thaws with its latent heat; and a massless skin on top closes the surface energy balance with the atmosphere. The result, SoilThermalColumn in src/SoilColumn.jl, is an AbstractSurface of the surface shared core.

Why it matters: without a ground heat flux the skin is decoupled from the heat stored below it. The single-slab step_soil! of the urban example once computed the conduction flux into the soil and never took it out of the skin; for the hot urban surfaces of that run the omission removed roughly half of the evening cooling (nearly all of it in deep canyons) — commit 2b31389. The flux works both ways: it drains a skin warmer than the soil beneath and, on a clear night over cooler air, feeds heat back to a radiatively cooling skin (section 5).

Since #18 the urban example has no soil model of its own. Since #129 the SoilThermalColumn is the surface of every street, building-adjacent and park column of the Alexanderplatz run: its massless skin exchanges Monin–Obukhov fluxes with the first air cell through step_surface!, with the soil water stepped (hydrology = true) so the moisture availability follows the top layer (see the Alexanderplatz run and docs/src/design/berlin_surface_radiation.md). Before that (#18) it sat under a surface slab, coupled through a contact conductance.

1. Thermal properties

Heat capacity is the sum over constituents (de Vries 1963),

\[C = c_s(1-\theta_s) + \rho_w c_w\,\theta_{liq} + \rho_{ice} c_{ice}\,\theta_{ice},\]

air neglected, with the solids' $c_s$ either from sand and clay ($c_s = (2.128\,\%sand + 2.385\,\%clay)/(\%sand+\%clay)\times10^6$ J m⁻³ K⁻¹; Farouki 1981, as in CLM) or the mineral value $2.0\times10^6$ J m⁻³ K⁻¹ of de Vries. $\rho_w$, $\rho_{ice}$ are the contract's SurfaceConstants; $c_w$, $c_{ice}$ are delegated through it to MoistThermodynamics.

Conductivity follows Johansen (1975) as used by CLM:

\[k = K_e\,k_{sat} + (1-K_e)\,k_{dry},\qquad k_{sat} = k_s^{1-\theta_s}\,k_w^{\theta_s f_{liq}}\,k_{ice}^{\theta_s(1-f_{liq})},\qquad k_{dry} = \frac{0.135\,\rho_d + 64.7}{\rho_p - 0.947\,\rho_d},\]

with $\rho_d = \rho_p(1-\theta_s)$, $f_{liq}$ the liquid fraction of the pore water, and the Kersten number $K_e$ a function of the degree of saturation $S_r = (\theta_{liq}+\theta_{ice})/\theta_s$: $\log_{10}S_r + 1$ (fine soils) or $0.7\log_{10}S_r + 1$ (coarse), floored at zero, when unfrozen, and $S_r$ when frozen. A partly frozen layer takes the liquid-weighted mean of the two — continuous, where CLM switches at 0 °C. Solids: $k_s = (8.80\,\%sand + 2.92\,\%clay)/(\%sand+\%clay)$ (Farouki; clm_soil_thermal_properties) or $7.7^q\,k_o^{1-q}$ from the quartz fraction (Johansen; Peters-Lidard et al. 1998; johansen_soil_thermal_properties).

For a 40 % sand / 20 % clay soil of porosity 0.45: $k_{dry}$ = 0.205, $k_{sat}$ = 2.24 unfrozen and 4.12 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ frozen (ice conducts 3.9 times better than water).

2. Freezing

Clapeyron curve. Liquid water held in pores next to ice has the matric potential (Koopmans & Miller 1966)

\[\psi_{fr}(T) = \frac{L_f\,(T - T_f)}{g\,T},\]

about −125 m (−1.22 MPa) per kelvin of undercooling. The largest liquid content that can coexist with ice is the retention curve evaluated there, $\theta_{liq,max}(T) = \theta(\psi_{fr}(T))$ — the "freezing = drying" analogy used by CLM5 (Niu & Yang 2006) and Dall'Amico et al. (2011). A layer at potential $\psi$ starts to freeze at $T^* = L_f T_f/(L_f - g\psi)$, below $T_f$; drier soil starts later. For the Carsel–Parrish loam the unfrozen water is 0.226, 0.120, 0.090 and 0.083 m³ m⁻³ at −0.01, −0.1, −1 and −5 °C.

freezing_point_depression = false gives instead a sharp phase change at $T_f$ — the classical Stefan problem. In both modes a small fraction (unfrozen_fraction, default 10⁻³) of the pore space above the residual content stays liquid, so the water solver always has a liquid phase to move.

Ice in the water solver. The retention curve of the water column is applied to the total water $W = \theta_{liq} + (\rho_{ice}/\rho_w)\theta_{ice}$ (after CLM4.5 onward, Swenson et al. 2012, which use the liquid-plus-ice volume; see the end of this paragraph): freezing moves water between phases at constant $\psi$, and ice acts through the impedance factor $10^{-\Omega\,\theta_{ice}/\theta_s}$ with $\Omega = 6$ (PowerLawIceImpedance) and the viscosity of cold water, rather than through a suction. The alternative — ψ from the liquid alone — makes a frozen layer look like very dry soil (−1250 m at −10 °C) next to wet unfrozen soil, and with the column's logarithmic interface conductivity that drives an unbounded "cryosuction" flux on coarse layers. Ice lensing and frost heave are not modelled; the liquid-equivalent $W$ is used rather than $\theta_{liq}+\theta_{ice}$ so that the 9 % expansion of freezing water does not register as over-saturation.

3. Enthalpy formulation

Each layer carries its volumetric enthalpy relative to liquid water at $T_f$,

\[H = C(\theta_{liq},\theta_{ice})\,(T - T_f) - \rho_{ice} L_f\,\theta_{ice},\]

which — with $L_f$ from MoistThermodynamics and constant specific heats — is Kirchhoff-consistent: the latent heat at $T$ is $L_f + (c_w - c_{ice})(T - T_f)$. Given $H$ and $W$, the temperature and the phase partition are the unique equilibrium: all liquid if $H \ge C_{liq}(T^*-T_f)$; otherwise the monotone equation $H_{eq}(T) = H$ is solved below $T^*$ by a safeguarded Newton iteration converged in enthalpy (to 10⁻¹³ of the latent heat content); in sharp mode the layer sits at $T_f$ while $H$ crosses the latent interval.

Discretisation. Finite volumes on the soil grid, with the interface conductance of the two half layers in series, and backward Euler:

\[\Delta z_i \frac{H_i^{n+1} - H_i^n}{\Delta t} = F_{i-1/2} - F_{i+1/2},\qquad F_{i+1/2} = \frac{T_i - T_{i+1}}{(z_{h,i+1}-z_i)/k_i + (z_{i+1}-z_{h,i+1})/k_{i+1}} .\]

The nonlinear system is solved by Newton's method in H with the conductivity lagged: $T \approx T^m + s^m(H - H^m)$, $s = dT/dH$. In a sharp phase change $s = 0$, so no apparent-heat-capacity smoothing is needed and the iteration cannot overshoot the latent plateau (the enthalpy method; Voller et al. 1990, Nedjar 2002). The matrix $D + AS$ ($A$ the conduction operator, $S = \mathrm{diag}(s)$) is column diagonally dominant, so the tridiagonal solve is stable. After convergence the new enthalpy is formed from the divergence of the converged fluxes, so $\Delta\sum\Delta z H = (G - F_{bottom})\Delta t$ holds to round-off. Sub-steps halve on non-convergence and grow after fast convergence.

Boundaries. Every upper condition is reduced to a ground flux linear in the top-node temperature, $G = \alpha + \beta T_1$: a prescribed flux (GroundHeatFluxTop), a prescribed skin temperature (SkinTemperatureTop, through the half layer $z_1$), or a linearised skin energy balance (LinearizedSkinBalance, below). The base is insulated (CLM's default), has a geothermal flux, or a fixed temperature.

Water. A Richards step keeps each layer's temperature while its liquid changes, so water carries the enthalpy of the layer it enters or leaves (booked as budget.water); each layer then re-equilibrates at constant enthalpy — water that infiltrates frozen soil refreezes and warms it. Rain is assumed to arrive at the temperature of the top layer.

4. The skin energy balance

step_surface!(col, forcing, dt):

  1. evaluates SurfaceExchange.turbulent_fluxes (Monin–Obukhov) at the current skin temperature $T_0$ and at $T_0 + 0.01$ K, giving $R_n$, $H$, $LE$, $E$ and their derivatives; evaporation is capped by the liquid in the top layer;
  2. solves the soil together with the skin, implicitly: $G_{atm}(T_s) = G_0 + (dG/dT_s)(T_s - T_0) = (k_1/z_1)(T_s - T_1)$ eliminates $T_s$ and becomes the linear upper condition of the heat solve;
  3. returns the fluxes at the step-mean skin temperature, so $R_n = H + LE + G$ exactly and $G\,\Delta t$ is exactly what the soil received;
  4. runs the Richards step with the rain of the forcing and the $E$ just returned.

The bare-soil evaporation efficiency is the Lee & Pielke (1992) β of the top layer's liquid water; roughness defaults to CLM4.5's bare-soil 0.01 m, emissivity to the dry-soil value of SurfaceOptics. The linearisation is done once per step, as in CLM.

5. Validation

All numbers are from test/soil_thermal.jl.

Neumann's freezing front (Carslaw & Jaeger 1959, §11.2). Soil with $W = 0.3$ at +2 °C, surface held at −10 °C, sharp freezing; the phase properties are the model's own for the fully frozen and thawed soil ($\lambda = 0.2897$). After 30 days the front, measured as the frozen-depth equivalent of the column's ice, is at

| Δz, Δt | numerical X | Neumann X | error | max $|ΔT|$ off the front cell | | :– | :– | :– | :– | :– | | 40 mm, 3600 s | 1.2595 m | 1.2653 m | −4.6e-3 | 0.027 K | | 20 mm, 900 s | 1.2625 m | 1.2653 m | −2.3e-3 | 0.058 K | | 10 mm, 225 s | 1.2639 m | 1.2653 m | −1.2e-3 | 0.012 K |

— first-order convergence of the front, as expected for a fixed-grid enthalpy method, and at most one partly frozen cell. The node of that cell is pinned at $T_f$ (the method's well-known staircase), which is why the temperature comparison leaves it out.

Periodic surface temperature (Carslaw & Jaeger §2.6). A sinusoidal skin temperature penetrates as $A e^{-z/d}\sin(\omega t - z/d)$ with damping depth $d = \sqrt{2k/(C\omega)}$, and the ground heat flux leads the surface temperature by π/4 with amplitude $A\sqrt{kC\omega}$. For the loam at $\theta = 0.3$ ($k$ = 2.02 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, $C$ = 2.52 MJ m⁻³ K⁻¹):

diurnal (Δz 5 mm, Δt 150 s)0.149 m0.3693, 0.13390.3703, 0.13480.996, 2.0020.7825

(π/4 = 0.7854; the ground-flux amplitude matches $A\sqrt{kC\omega}$ to 1e-4). The residual amplitude error has two parts: the numerical damping of backward Euler, and the start-up transient left after the six cycles the test runs. At 3d it is −4.2 %, −1.8 % and −1.2 % after 6 cycles, and −3.4 %, −1.0 % and −0.36 % after 16, for (Δz, Δt) = (10 mm, 600 s), (5 mm, 150 s), (2.5 mm, 37.5 s).

Energy conservation. Forty days of freezing and thawing a layered CLM5 column under ±80 W m⁻² with a geothermal base, both freezing modes: worst per-step residual 6e-8 J m⁻², cumulative 5e-8 J m⁻², water conserved to 1e-14. A single sharp-freezing layer holds exactly at $T_f$ (a zero curtain) for $\rho_{ice}L_f\theta_{ice,max}\Delta z/|G|$ and then cools. The full coupled surface–heat–water runs close $\Delta H = \int G - \int F_{bottom} + \text{water}$ to round-off.

Skin balance. $R_n - H - LE - G$ = 0 to 1e-10 W m⁻² every step. The linearisation error — the full Monin–Obukhov balance at the new skin temperature minus $G$ — is 13.8 W m⁻² on the first step of a night (the skin drops 3 K from an unbalanced start) and at most 0.21 W m⁻² from the fourth 10-minute step on. After 12 h of a clear night the conducting soil keeps the skin at 280.2 K, where a surface with $G = 0$ would sit at 272.5 K. Under constant forcing over a fixed-temperature base the column reaches the steady state $G = k\,(T_s - T_b)/D$ with the full nonlinear balance closed to 0.01 W m⁻².

Frozen ground. 30 mm of rain in 6 h: 0 mm runoff on thawed loam, 30 mm on frozen loam (ice impedance). Water infiltrating a frozen layer refreezes and warms it from −2.00 to −0.004 °C. A 60-day winter–spring cycle for every Carsel–Parrish texture builds and thaws frost with energy and water closed.

Not validated against observations. The phase target — soil temperature and moisture profiles at FLUXNET sites — needs the vegetated land column (#21); this issue is validated against the analytic solutions and conservation laws above only.
