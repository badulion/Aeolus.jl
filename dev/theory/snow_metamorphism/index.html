Snow metamorphism and albedo ageing

Phase 51 (#35) gives the layered snowpack a prognostic optical grain radius and lets its albedo follow it. The albedo physics itself is not new: it is the Wiscombe–Warren snow albedo that SurfaceOptics.SnowSurface already implements. This phase supplies the grain radius that model needs, and hands it over.

1. The optical grain radius

Snow is a porous ice matrix whose shortwave properties are fixed, to a good approximation, by one number: the specific surface area (SSA, m² kg⁻¹), or equivalently the radius of ice spheres with the same surface-to-mass ratio,

\[r = \frac{3}{ρ_{ice}\,\mathrm{SSA}}\]

(snow_specific_surface_area, snow_optical_radius; $ρ_{ice}$ is the contract's 917 kg m⁻³). Fresh snow has SSA ≈ 60 m² kg⁻¹, r ≈ 55 μm; old melt forms reach SSA ≈ 5 m² kg⁻¹, r > 600 μm (Carmagnola et al. 2014 measured 5–80 m² kg⁻¹ at Col de Porte). Each layer of LayeredSnowpack carries grain[i] in μm. New snow enters with fresh_grain_radius(scheme, pack, T_air) and is mass-weighted into the top layer; merging layers mass-weights the radius; refrozen melt enters through mix_refrozen_grain! (the refreezing itself belongs to #36).

2. Dry metamorphism: Flanner & Zender (2006)

Vapour moves from high- to low-curvature ice and, under a temperature gradient, from warm to cold grains. Flanner & Zender (2006) simulated this for ensembles of ice spheres and fitted the growth of the effective radius, for each temperature, temperature gradient and density, to

\[\frac{dr}{dt} = \left(\frac{dr}{dt}\right)_0 \left(\frac{τ}{(r - r_0) + τ}\right)^{1/κ}, \qquad r_0 = 54.5\ \mathrm{μm}.\]

The fits are CLM's look-up table snicar_drdt_bst_fit_60_c070416.nc (11 temperatures 223–273 K × 31 gradients 0–300 K m⁻¹ × 8 densities 50–400 kg m⁻³), shipped unchanged in src/data/ and read with NCDatasets (snicar_aging_table).

Exact integration. At fixed parameters the law integrates in closed form. With $x = r - r_0 + τ$ and $e = 1 + 1/κ$,

\[x(t)^{e} = x(0)^{e} + e\,(dr/dt)_0\,τ^{1/κ}\,t ,\]

(flanner_grain_radius). This composes exactly — ageing for $t_1$ then $t_2$ is ageing for $t_1 + t_2$ — so at fixed state and a single table node (interpolation = :nearest) the result does not depend on the time step. The default :linear blend of node outcomes (below) does not compose exactly: ten days in one step and in 480 half-hour steps differ by up to 0.9 % (108.7 vs 109.7 μm). CLM takes one forward-Euler step per model step instead; over 30 days of 30-minute steps the two differ by at most 0.1 % (338.37 vs 338.05 μm at −5 °C, 20 K m⁻¹, 100 kg m⁻³).

Interpolation. CLM uses the nearest table node (interpolation = :nearest). The three parameters are fitted jointly and trade off against one another, so interpolating them separately is wrong: between the 300 and 350 kg m⁻³ nodes at −25 °C and 50 K m⁻¹, where the nodes grow an 88 μm grain to 181 and 123 μm in 37 days, interpolated parameters give 484 μm. The default :linear therefore evaluates the exact solution at the eight surrounding nodes and interpolates the radii (snicar_grain_radius). The result always lies between the node outcomes, and the 15 K m⁻¹ cell edge that makes nearest-node growth jump by more than 1 μm over 10 days changes it by less than 1 % of that jump.

Temperature gradient. As in CLM (CLM5 Technical Note, Eq. 2.3.74), from interface temperatures that are thickness-weighted means of neighbouring layers, with the top interface taken at the top-layer temperature. The bottom interface is the ground temperature (SnowGroundTemperature), or the temperature that carries the prescribed flux across half the bottom layer (SnowBottomFlux) (snow_temperature_gradient).

Trends. Flanner & Zender report that warm snow, a strong temperature gradient and low density age fastest. Over the table the 30-day growth rises with temperature in 98.1 % of adjacent cells, with gradient in 93.0 % and falls with density in 94.2 %. The exceptions come from the fits. At 100 kg m⁻³ and no gradient, 30-day radii run from 69 μm at −50 °C to 133 μm at 0 °C. At −10 °C, 20 K m⁻¹ takes the radius to 296 μm.

3. Wet metamorphism: Brun (1989)

Liquid water coarsens grains much faster. Brun (1989) measured growth rates against liquid-water content. CLM uses them without the dry offset:

\[\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{10^{18}\,C_1\,f_{liq}^3}{4π r^2}\ \mathrm{μm\,s^{-1}}, \qquad C_1 = 4.22·10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}},\]

with $f_{liq}$ the liquid mass fraction, capped at 0.1 as in CTSM. In $r^3$ this is linear in time, so it too is integrated exactly. At $f_{liq} = 0.1$ a 54.5 μm grain reaches 443 μm in ten days. In the same ten days, dry ageing at 0 °C, 100 kg m⁻³ and no gradient takes it to 108 μm.

With the default InstantSnowRunoff liquid-water scheme of #34, no liquid stays in the pack, so wet growth only acts once #36 retains water.

4. Albedo: delegation to SurfaceOptics.SnowSurface

SnowOpticsAlbedo computes the thickness-weighted grain radius of the top sensing_depth (2 cm) with snowpack_optical_radius. It builds SnowSurface(spec; grain_radius = r, soot_ppmw) and returns broadband_albedo, diffuse by default or direct-beam at a given $μ_0$. SnowSurface holds the asymptotic Wiscombe–Warren parameterisation, $α = \exp(-\sqrt{\ln^2 α_{clean} + s})$ with $α_{clean}(r) = α(r_0)^{\sqrt{r/r_0}}$. The snowpack module does not restate it. snowpack_optics returns that SnowSurface, so band-resolved radiation code sees the same surface. Because the albedo enters SurfaceExchange.net_radiation through surface_albedo, grain growth feeds back on the energy balance. At 600 W m⁻², growing the grains from 54.5 to 500 μm adds 64 W m⁻² of absorbed sunshine.

Grain growth darkens the near-infrared, where ice absorbs. From 54.5 to 300 μm the VIS (< 0.7 μm) albedo falls by 1.8 % and the NIR albedo by 22.6 %, a ratio of 12. In the visible the albedo is set by impurities (soot_ppmw).

What these comparisons are, stated plainly:

  • Summit is the only comparison with observations. The paper reports the SSA at 2–15 cm depth, 37 ± 3 m² kg⁻¹ over 5–19 May 2011 and 23 ± 3 m² kg⁻¹ over 11–25 June, at 315–335 kg m⁻³. It does not report the snow temperature or temperature gradient over that interval, so the test scans an assumed cold ice-sheet range and asks only that the observation fall inside the model's spread. It does, and near the centre of the range. Without a gradient (−15 °C) the model gives 26.7 m² kg⁻¹, just above the observed 23 ± 3.
  • Albedo decay is compared with reference models, not data: the US Army Corps of Engineers (1956) curves as coded in VIC, ECMWF (Douville et al. 1995), CLASS (Verseghy 1991) and BATS (Dickinson et al. 1993) as coded in Noah-MP. These schemes were tuned to real, dirty snow. BATS's cold-snow ageing, for example, is dominated by its constant "dirt and soot" term. From day 5 on, clean-snow grain growth darkens more slowly than every one of them: at day 30 the pack is at 0.770 against 0.55–0.64. (On day 1 it is not: fresh grains coarsen fastest, 0.851 → 0.835, below ECMWF's linear 0.842.) The test asserts this as a bound, not as agreement. With 0.05 ppmw of black carbon the pack reaches 0.730. Observed melting snow at Col de Porte goes below 0.5 (Carmagnola et al. 2014). Getting there needs refrozen grains (#36) and impurity deposition and melt enrichment, which are not modelled: soot_ppmw is a fixed parameter.
  • No SnowMIP site forcing is used.
