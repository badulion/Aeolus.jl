Boussinesq equations

The Boussinesq approximation is a controlled simplification of the compressible Navier–Stokes system that is standard for boundary-layer flows in the atmosphere, ocean, and much of engineering CFD. It rests on two observations:

  1. Density variations across the flow are small compared to a mean ρ₀. Formally, Δρ/ρ₀ ≪ 1.
  2. Those small density variations matter only where they multiply gravity — i.e. in the buoyancy term. Everywhere else we can treat the fluid as incompressible with density ρ₀.

Applying these two rules to the compressible momentum equation yields:

\[\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} = -\frac{1}{\rho_0}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_i} - \frac{\rho'}{\rho_0}\,g\,\delta_{i3} + \nu \nabla^2 u_i\]

paired with the incompressibility constraint

\[\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_j} = 0.\]

Why Boussinesq for boundary layers

For atmospheric turbulence and boundary-layer meteorology:

  • Vertical scales are typically ≤ 2–3 km, well within the shallow limit where the anelastic corrections are small.
  • Mach numbers are tiny; acoustic modes are irrelevant.
  • Buoyancy is the dominant density effect and is the only one that survives to drive convection.

For the ocean, the Boussinesq approximation is even better justified — seawater density variations are ~0.1 %.

For engineering flows (heated channels, chimneys, indoor ventilation), the same approximation applies whenever βΔT ≪ 1 with β the thermal expansion coefficient.

Buoyancy from potential temperature

For a dry atmosphere the density anomaly ρ'/ρ₀ is related to the potential temperature anomaly by:

\[\frac{\rho'}{\rho_0} \approx -\frac{\theta - \theta_0}{\theta_0}\]

(θ is the temperature a fluid parcel would have if brought adiabatically to a reference pressure). This gives the buoyancy term we implement in the w-momentum equation:

\[b = g\,\frac{\theta - \theta_0}{\theta_0}.\]

Concretely: add_buoyancy! interpolates θ from cell centers to the z-face and adds b to the w-tendency.

Rotation — the f-plane

For flows over horizontal scales of tens of km or more, the Coriolis force is not negligible. Aeolus uses the f-plane approximation:

\[\text{Coriolis} = -f\,\hat z \times u = (+f\,v,\; -f\,u,\; 0)\]

with a constant f = 2Ω sin(φ) where Ω is Earth's rotation rate and φ is latitude. This is standard for LES boxes small enough that curvature can be ignored (typically < 100 km).

Implemented by add_coriolis!, which uses a 4-point horizontal average to interpolate v to the u-face and vice versa — preserving the discrete rotational symmetry.

What "incompressible" costs us

Because there is no evolution equation for p, the pressure is a Lagrange multiplier that enforces ∇·u = 0 at every instant. On the continuum this is elegant; on a discrete grid it means we must solve a Poisson equation every time step (see Fractional-step projection). The FFT solver we use is exact on the discrete operator, so this is not an approximation — just a computational cost.

What we don't include (yet)

  • Non-hydrostatic compressibility corrections (anelastic / pseudo-incompressible systems). Aeolus is purely Boussinesq — deep convection with strong density variation is out of scope.
  • Moisture — the water-vapor and cloud-microphysics equations are a Phase 2 target.
  • Radiation — Phase 5.
