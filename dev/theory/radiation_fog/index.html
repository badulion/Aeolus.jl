Radiation fog

This page covers the radiative half of radiation fog in Aeolus (Era IV, Phase 44, issue #9): how fog droplets enter the Era III radiation, why that coupling is what thickens a fog, and the 1-D column used to test it against the ParisFog case of 14–15 November 2011. The thermodynamic half (condensation, dew, energy conservation) is in Fog and dew. The code is in src/radiation_fog/ inside the RadiationFog module.

1. The feedback

A clear night cools the ground by about 60 W m⁻² of net longwave (Wærsted et al. 2017 measured this at SIRTA before fog formed). Once fog forms and its liquid water path passes about 30 g m⁻², it becomes opaque in the thermal infrared. The ground then sees the fog's own emission instead of the cold sky, and its net longwave drops toward zero. The cooling moves to the fog top, which now radiates to the sky with a net loss of 70–100 W m⁻². That cooling is concentrated in the uppermost 50–100 m of the fog. Saturated air cooled there condenses more water, and cooling from the top destabilises the fog layer. Mixing then carries the cold, wet air down and the fog deepens. Without the droplets in the radiation none of this happens: the fog stays a thin layer that cools only from the ground.

2. Coupling to the Era III radiation

Nothing in RadiationFog computes an optical property. For each layer the column writes

  • the fog water q_l into AtmosphericColumn.ql,
  • the effective radius $r_\mathrm{eff} = \bigl(3\,\mathrm{LWC}/(4\pi\rho_w N_d k)\bigr)^{1/3}$ (from AerosolCloud.droplet_effective_radius, Martin et al. 1994, $k = 0.8$) into r_eff_liq,
  • the vapour into vmr[:h2o].

It then calls GasOptics correlated-k, TwoStream.MieCloudOptics, RadiationCore.combine_optics! and solve_lw! / solve_sw!. Above the 1 km model top sits a fixed us_standard_column upper atmosphere, made continuous in temperature, pressure and humidity with the model top, so that the sky seen from the fog top has realistic emission.

droplet_feedback = false passes ql = 0 to the radiation. The fog still exists thermodynamically (its water, its latent heat, its stability) but is transparent. Switching it off is the controlled experiment behind the feedback tests.

Radiation-driven condensation

Wærsted et al. (2017, Eqs. 1–5) turn a radiative heating rate into the condensation it forces in saturated air:

\[C_\mathrm{rad} = -\frac{\frac{d\rho_s}{dT}\left(\frac{dT}{dt}\right)_\mathrm{rad}} {1 + \frac{L_v}{\rho c_p}\frac{d\rho_s}{dT}},\qquad \frac{d\rho_s}{dT} = \frac{e_s}{R_v T^2}\left(\frac{L_v}{R_v T} - 1\right).\]

radiative_condensation_rate evaluates it with the contract's $e_s$ and $L_v$. It gives 0.525 g m⁻² h⁻¹ per W m⁻² at −2 °C and 0.897 at 15 °C. Wærsted et al. quote 0.55 and 0.90. It also agrees within 2.6 % with the exact outcome of fog_condensation applied to a parcel cooled by the same energy.

3. The column

FogColumn is a single-column model with 1 m layers at the ground, stretched by 8 % per layer to at most 25 m, up to 1000 m. Each step of 10 s does the following:

  1. Radiation, every 60 s, with gas optics refreshed every 15 min. The heating is applied as $\Delta h = Q\,\Delta t/\rho$ at fixed water partition.
  2. Surface. A force-restore ground temperature (Bhumralkar 1975; Deardorff 1978) with admittance 800 J m⁻² K⁻¹ s⁻½, receiving the net longwave (upwelling re-evaluated from the current $T_s$), the absorbed shortwave, a bulk sensible heat flux and the latent heat of dew_deposition or of β-limited soil evaporation.
  3. Turbulence. A first-order K closure, $K = l^2 |\partial U/\partial z| f(Ri) + K_\min$, with the Blackadar length ($\lambda$ = 30 m) and $f = (1+5Ri)^{-2}$ for $Ri > 0$, $\sqrt{1-16Ri}$ for $Ri < 0$ (the ShortTail family of the Era VI stability contract, TurbulenceStability, since #170). $Ri$ uses a moist $N^2$, measured by displacing a parcel across each interface with conserved $s = h + gz$ and total water, saturation-adjusting it with fog_equilibrium, and comparing $\theta_\rho$. In an 8 K km⁻¹ layer this gives $N^2 = +5.4\times10^{-5}$ s⁻² dry and $-6.4\times10^{-6}$ s⁻² saturated. That sign change is what lets fog-top cooling overturn the fog. Mixing is implicit and in flux form for $s$, $q_v$, $q_l$ and momentum, followed by Coriolis turning toward an 8 m s⁻¹ geostrophic wind.
  4. Settling at one bulk speed, Superdroplet.terminal_velocity evaluated at $r_\mathrm{eff}$: 1.35 cm s⁻¹ at 0.2 g m⁻³ and 50 cm⁻³. A fall-speed spectrum belongs to issue #8.
  5. Saturation adjustment with fog_condensation.

Conservation. Mixing and settling move static energy in flux form, and the phase change conserves moist enthalpy exactly. The column integral $\sum \rho\Delta z\,(h + gz)$ therefore changes only through the radiative net flux at the model top and surface, the sensible heat flux, and the enthalpy of the vapour exchanged with the ground and of the droplets that settle out. FogBudget accumulates all four. Over 6 h of the case the residual is 5×10⁻⁶ J m⁻² on a 1.19×10⁶ J m⁻² change, and over the full 14 h it is 4×10⁻¹² of the summed boundary fluxes. Water closes to ~10⁻¹².

Time step. The eddy diffusivity is evaluated from the start-of-step state and then used in an implicit solve. With a 1 m lowest layer this develops the well-known 2Δt oscillation ("fibrillation") of stability-dependent K closures (Kalnay & Kanamitsu 1988) once dt ≳ 10 s. At dt = 10 s the surface is 0.32 K colder after 3 h than at dt = 2.5 s, and fog reaches the ground 45 min earlier. At dt = 20 s the onset moves by 3.5 h. The default is therefore dt = 5 s, which agrees with dt = 2.5 s to 0.006 K after 3 h (a test) and with dt = 1 s to 10 min in onset and 5 min in clearance over the 14 h case.

4. Validation: ParisFog, 14–15 November 2011

Mazoyer et al. (2017) document a radiation fog at SIRTA (48.71 °N, 2.21 °E). Liquid water appeared at 150 m at 02:00 UTC, and fog reached the ground at 02:30. The fog was optically thick by ≈ 03:00 at ≈ 80 m depth, its LWP peaked at ≈ 70 g m⁻² at 07:30 and its top at ≤ 300 m (sodar). It cleared at the ground at ≈ 10:00, three hours after the 06:59 sunrise. Their LES starts at 23:20 UTC with a 276 K surface and an 8 m s⁻¹ geostrophic wind, and so does parisfog_2011_column.

What is not reproduced. The initial sounding is only shown as a figure, so the column's profile is constructed, not digitised. It has a 2 K inversion over 60 m, a weakly stable layer to 400 m, relative humidity 97 % at the ground falling to 88 % at 400 m and 50 % above, and 4 K km⁻¹ above. The profile was set before the first run and not tuned afterwards. Tree drag and droplet deposition on vegetation, which Mazoyer et al. found to matter most for their LES, are absent. Onset and depth are therefore compared to the observations as a life cycle, not fitted to them.

$C_\mathrm{LW}$ falls below the observed range. The conversion factor is right (0.58–0.60 g m⁻² h⁻¹ per W m⁻² at 275 K, where Wærsted et al. give 0.55 to 0.90 between −2 °C and 15 °C), so the shortfall is in the radiative loss itself. The fog top loses 63–68 W m⁻² rather than 73–100. Two causes are identifiable. First, the constructed air above the fog is humid (88 % to 400 m), which strengthens the downwelling longwave. Second, MieCloudOptics gives fog a longwave single-scattering albedo of 0.45, which makes its emissivity ≈ 0.95 rather than ≈ 1: the downwelling at the ground under the fog is 314 W m⁻² against 326 W m⁻² for a black body at the fog temperature, and Mazoyer et al. observed 325 W m⁻². The result sits inside Nakanishi's (2000) LES estimate of ≈ 30–40 g m⁻² h⁻¹, as quoted by Wærsted et al. The peak cooling rate depends on resolution, because the fog top here sits in 10–25 m layers. The resolution-independent quantity is the flux jump.

With the feedback off the ground keeps cooling at the clear-sky rate and the fog remains a weak, radiatively passive layer.

Dissipation and the sun

With the sun switched off, the fog still leaves the ground at 08:05, 5 minutes later than with the sun. The ground heat flux, restoring the surface toward the deeper soil, and top-driven mixing lift its base. The layer then persists, with 73 g m⁻² at 13:20. With the sun on, the surface absorbs up to 157 W m⁻² under the lifted fog while its LWP is still above 30 g m⁻² (Wærsted et al.: net radiation 40–120 W m⁻² at a 70° zenith angle; the sun here reaches ≈ 67°), and the layer is gone by 12:50. In this setup, then, the sun removes the fog layer. Clearing at the ground is set mostly by the surface heat balance, and the tests assert only what the model shows.
