Stomatal conductance

Stomata are pores that let CO₂ into a leaf, and water vapour out through the same opening. The leaf photosynthesis page gives the assimilation rate $A_n(c_i)$ at a given intercellular CO₂. This page closes the loop: how open the stomata are, the CO₂ inside the leaf that results, and the transpiration that comes with it. The code is the second half of src/Photosynthesis.jl (Phase 47, #20).

1. Diffusion paths

CO₂ travels from the air ($c_a$) across the leaf boundary layer to the leaf surface ($c_s$), then through the stomatal pore to the intercellular spaces ($c_i$). With no storage at the surface, the three fluxes are equal (CLM5 eq. 2.9.19):

\[A_n = \frac{c_a - c_i}{1.4/g_b + 1.6/g_s} = \frac{g_b}{1.4}(c_a - c_s) = \frac{g_s}{1.6}(c_s - c_i).\]

Here $g_s$ and $g_b$ are the stomatal and boundary-layer conductances to water vapour, in mol m⁻² s⁻¹. 1.6 is the ratio of the molecular diffusivities of H₂O and CO₂ in air. 1.4 ≈ 1.6^{2/3} is the same ratio for a laminar boundary layer, where the dependence on diffusivity is weaker. With CO₂ in µmol mol⁻¹ the flux is in µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and pressure drops out.

Water vapour leaves along the same path in reverse. The leaf interior is saturated at leaf temperature, $e_i = e_{sat}(T_\ell)$, where $e_{sat}$ is MoistThermodynamics.saturation_vapor_pressure, so

\[E = \frac{e_i - e_a}{p}\,\frac{g_s g_b}{g_s + g_b},\qquad e_s = \frac{g_b e_a + g_s e_i}{g_b + g_s}\]

(CLM5 eqs. 2.9.20, 2.9.23). The humidity at the leaf surface therefore depends on $g_s$ itself: opening the stomata humidifies the leaf surface.

Boundary layer

For forced convection over a leaf of dimension $d$ the boundary-layer conductance scales as $\sqrt{U/d}$ (laminar flat plate). CLM5 (eq. 2.5.122) takes

\[g_b = C_v \sqrt{U/d_{leaf}},\qquad C_v = 0.01\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1/2}},\quad d_{leaf} = 0.04\ \mathrm{m},\]

and converts it to molar units with $p/(RT)$. At $U = 1$ m s⁻¹ and 20 °C this is 2.08 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, typically ten times the stomatal conductance. That is why well-ventilated leaves are close to $c_s ≈ c_a$.

2. Stomatal closures

Ball-Berry (Ball, Woodrow & Berry 1987; Collatz et al. 1991; CLM4.5):

\[g_s = g_0 + g_1\,\frac{A_n h_s}{c_s},\]

where $h_s = e_s/e_i$ is the relative humidity at the leaf surface. Substituting $e_s(g_s)$ gives a quadratic in $g_s$:

\[g_s^2 + (g_b - g_0 - a)\,g_s - g_b\,(g_0 + a\,e_a/e_i) = 0,\qquad a = g_1 A_n / c_s .\]

This is the quadratic of CLM's ci_func. The product of its roots is negative, so it has exactly one positive root, and the code evaluates that root in a form free of cancellation. CLM defaults: $g_1 = 9$, $g_0 = 0.01$ mol m⁻² s⁻¹ for C3; $g_1 = 4$, $g_0 = 0.04$ for C4.

Medlyn (Medlyn et al. 2011; CLM5 eq. 2.9.1). This form comes from optimal stomatal behaviour, which maximises carbon gain minus a marginal water cost:

\[g_s = g_0 + 1.6\left(1 + \frac{g_1}{\sqrt{D_s}}\right)\frac{A_n}{c_s},\]

with $D_s$ in kPa. Since $D_s = e_i - e_s = g_b D_a/(g_b + g_s)$, the substitution $x = \sqrt{g_b + g_s}$ turns the closure into

\[x^2 - c_2 x - (g_b + g_0 + c_1) = 0,\qquad c_1 = 1.6 A_n / c_s,\quad c_2 = g_1 c_1 / \sqrt{g_b D_a},\]

which again has exactly one positive root. Squaring the closure instead gives CLM5's quadratic (eqs. 2.9.24–2.9.26). Its larger root is the same $g_s$; the tests check agreement to 3 × 10⁻¹⁶.

For $g_0 = 0$ and $g_b → ∞$ the Medlyn closure implies

\[\frac{c_i}{c_a} = \frac{g_1}{g_1 + \sqrt{D}},\]

independent of $A_n$, light, temperature and $c_a$. This is the model's defining prediction and the strongest analytic test of the coupled solver.

No net uptake. For $A_n ≤ 0$ both closures return $g_0$. Both are continuous at $A_n = 0$.

Deficit bound (a deliberate difference from CLM5). The Medlyn closure diverges as $D_s → 0$. CLM5 bounds the air deficit $e_i - e_a$ at 50 Pa. That leaves the leaf-surface deficit unbounded for a weakly coupled leaf, where the humidification feedback can run away: at 5 °C, 98 % RH and $g_b = 0.1$ mol m⁻² s⁻¹ the tests found $g_s ≈ 10^3$ mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Aeolus evaluates the closure at $\max(D_s, D_{min})$ with $D_{min}$ = 50 Pa. This caps $g_s$ and is identical to CLM5 whenever $D_s ≥ D_{min}$.

3. The coupled solution

Given $c_i$, the four relations determine everything else: $A_n = f(c_i)$ from the Farquhar or Collatz biochemistry, $c_s = c_a - 1.4A_n/g_b$, $g_s$ from the closure, and a new intercellular CO₂. The solution is the root of

\[R(c_i) = c_i - \left[c_a - A_n(c_i)\left(\frac{1.4}{g_b} + \frac{1.6}{g_s}\right)\right].\]

$R(0) < 0$, because a leaf cannot fix carbon with no CO₂ inside. At the upper end $c_i = c_a$, $R ≥ 0$ whenever the leaf has net uptake there. Otherwise, as in darkness, the root lies above $c_a$, at the $c_i$ that respiration implies. leaf_gas_exchange brackets the root and solves it with the Illinois method, the same routine SurfaceExchange uses for Monin–Obukhov. It is the bracketed counterpart of CLM5's hybrid secant/Brent solver, which replaced CLM4's fixed-point iteration because that iteration did not always converge (Sun et al. 2012, cited in CLM5 §2.9.6). Typical cases need 5–20 iterations. Near the compensation point up to ~100 are needed, because $g_s$ drops to $g_0$ as $A_n$ changes sign and $R$ becomes steep there.

4. Soil-water stress

The plant wetness factor $β ∈ [0, 1]$ (CLM's btran) is an input. The land column (#21) computes it from soil matric potential. apply_water_stress multiplies it into the parameters. By default it scales $V_{cmax}$, $R_d$ and $g_0$. That is what CLM4.5 does, and what CLM5's non-hydraulic Ball-Berry path does. CLM5's Medlyn closure (ci_func_PHS, which runs with plant hydraulics) keeps its intercept unstressed. Scaling the Medlyn $g_0$ by β is therefore an Aeolus choice, and a small one, since $g_0 = 10^{-4}$ mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Use stress_targets = (:vcmax, :rd) to match CLM5 there. It can also scale the slope $g_1$ (stress_targets = (:g1,)), which is the alternative used in CABLE-style studies. The stomatal intercept is always at least 1 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, CLM's floor, so the system stays well posed at $β = 0$.
