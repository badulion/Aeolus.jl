Lake and sea ice

Floating ice is a slab that loses heat upward by conduction, gains it from the water below, and changes thickness wherever the two do not balance. This page develops the model in src/IceThermodynamics.jl: the energy-conserving multi-layer thermodynamics of Bitz & Lipscomb (1999, "BL99"), which reduces exactly to pure lake ice when the salinity is zero.

1. Sea ice is a mushy layer

Sea ice holds brine in pockets that stay at the liquidus, $T_m = -μS$ (°C, $μ$ = 0.054 K ppt⁻¹; Assur 1958). Warming the ice melts the pocket walls, so the effective heat capacity is (Untersteiner 1961; Ono 1967)

\[c(T,S) = c_0 + \frac{L\,μ S}{T^2},\]

which exceeds $c_0$ thirty-fold at -0.5 °C for S = 3.2 ppt. Integrating $ρc$ from $T$ to the liquidus gives the energy of melting, the energy that turns a unit volume into brine (BL99 eq. 5):

\[q(S,T) = ρ_i\left[c_0 (T_m - T) + L\left(1 - \frac{T_m}{T}\right)\right].\]

For $S = 0$ it is the familiar $ρ_i[c_0(0 - T) + L]$; for $S > 0$ it falls to zero at $T = T_m$. At -2 °C and 3.2 ppt it is $0.925\,ρ_i L$ (BL99 quote 0.92). The conductivity is lowered by the brine, $k = k_0 + βS/T$ (Untersteiner 1964, β = 0.1172 W m⁻¹).

BL99's point: a model that stores heat in brine pockets through $c(T,S)$ but melts the surface with $q = ρ_i L$ counts that heat twice; it over-predicts the MU71 equilibrium thickness by 50–124 cm.

2. Enthalpy form and the discretisation

Each of the $N$ equal ice layers carries its enthalpy $E = -q$ (J m⁻³, zero for brine at the liquidus); the snow layer carries $E_s = ρ_s(c_0 T - L)$. The heat equation

\[\frac{∂E}{∂t} = \frac{∂}{∂z}\left(k \frac{∂T}{∂z}\right) + I_0 κ e^{-κ z}\]

(penetrating shortwave $I_0 = i_0(1-α)SW↓$ on snow-free ice, Beer's law with $κ$ = 1.5 m⁻¹) is stepped backward-Euler. For given conductances the new temperatures solve a tridiagonal system, nonlinear through $E(T)$; Newton linearises $E$ with $ρc(T,S)$ and, when the surface is not melting, the surface balance

\[F(T_s) = (1-α)(1-i_0)SW↓ + εLW↓ - εσT_s^4 - H(T_s) - LE(T_s) = F_{c}(T_s, T_1)\]

with its derivative. When $F$ at the melting point exceeds the conductive flux, $T_s$ is pinned there and the surplus melts the surface. Layers driven past their liquidus are pinned at it.

The enthalpies are then updated from the interface fluxes actually used, $E_j ← E_j + Δt(F_{j+1/2} - F_{j-1/2} + A_j)/Δz_j$. Energy is therefore conserved to round-off whatever the Newton tolerance; the temperature is only an intermediate. Melting consumes exactly $-E$ per unit volume (snow first, then ice from the top; from the bottom when the ocean flux $F_w$ exceeds the basal conduction $F_{cb}$); basal accretion adds ice of enthalpy $E(T_f, S_{new})$ at the rate

\[\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{F_{cb} - F_w}{q(T_f, S_{new})}.\]

Snow pushed below the waterline ($ρ_i h + ρ_s h_s > ρ_w h$) becomes ice, keeping mass and enthalpy. Finally the layers are remapped conservatively to $N$ equal layers under a salinity profile fixed in relative depth (BL99). The skin, snow and ice interfaces use half-cell fluxes ($2k/Δz$) and harmonic-mean conductances.

3. Stefan's law and the Neumann solution

With the surface held $ΔT$ below the freezing point and no ocean heat, a linear (quasi-steady) profile gives Stefan's (1891) law

\[h^2 = h_0^2 + \frac{2kΔT}{ρ_i L}\,t ,\]

the basis of degree-day lake-ice forecasts. Keeping the sensible heat of the ice gives the exact Neumann solution $h = 2λ\sqrt{αt}$ with $λe^{λ^2}\mathrm{erf}\,λ = St/\sqrt{π}$, $St = c_0ΔT/L$, $λ ≈ \sqrt{St/2}(1 - St/6)$: for ΔT = 10 K the ice is 1.0 % thinner than Stefan predicts.

4. The MU71 benchmark

Maykut & Untersteiner (1971) forced a column with Fletcher's (1965) climatological fluxes at ~80 °N, 2 W m⁻² from the ocean and a snowfall schedule; BL99 (and Semtner 1976) use the same protocol. The forcing, as distributed with the BL99 reference code, is in test/data/mu71_forcing.csv.

Against the BL99 reference code (run in GNU Octave 11.1, same configuration: N = 10, Δt = 12 h, and the code's own $c_0$ = 2054 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹): the first annual cycle agrees to a few millimetres (mean/max/min 263.8/293.0/242.5 cm vs 264.1/293.6/242.8). Over decades the two part: the reference settles at 265 cm, this model at ~250 cm. The cause is one boundary stencil. With N = 10 the reference code estimates the conductive flux at a snow surface by a quadratic through the snow midpoint and the first ice-layer midpoint — across the snow/ice interface, where $k$ jumps from 0.31 to ~2 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ and the profile has a kink a quadratic cannot represent. Replacing that one stencil by the half-cell flux used here brings the reference code to 248.5 cm after 20 years (ours 250.4 cm; all-first-order reference 250.0 cm). The unmodified reference also drifts towards our value as its layers are refined (265.7, 259.1, 254.7 cm at N = 10, 30, 60, year 20), while this model changes by 1.5 cm (250.4, 249.5, 249.0 cm).

The same holds in the published Table-1 configuration (Δt = 4 h, i₀ through snow after Bettge et al. 1996, $T_f$ = −1.95 °C, 100 years): the unmodified reference code gives 281.0 cm — BL99's published 281 — and 262.6 cm with only the snow-surface stencil changed; this model gives 264.2 cm.

5. Coupling

IceColumn implements SurfaceExchange.AbstractSurface: skin temperature, roughness lengths, albedo (dry snow 0.80, melting snow 0.75, bare ice 0.63, open water 0.06), emissivity, saturation over ice and the latent heat of sublimation. step_surface! iterates the Monin–Obukhov fluxes inside the Newton solve, so the returned $R_n - H - LE - G$ closes to ~1e-12 W m⁻², and removes the sublimated mass (snow first) with its enthalpy. Open water freezes over when it loses heat at its freezing point; ice thinner than 0.1 mm melts into the water. Rain is assumed to drain without exchanging heat.
