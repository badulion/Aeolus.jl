Occupancy schedules and BESTEST validation

A building's energy demand depends on how it is used as much as on how it is built. People, lights and office equipment release heat on a weekly rhythm, and the thermostat follows the same rhythm. Issue #33 adds these schedules to src/BuildingEnergy.jl and checks the whole zone model (conduction, air node, infiltration, internal gains, ideal loads) against the building-energy benchmark ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 140-2020 (BESTEST).

1. Schedules

A DayProfile is a piecewise-constant day in the form of an EnergyPlus Schedule:Compact day: value $v_i$ holds from breakpoint $u_{i-1}$ to $u_i$ hours ($u_0 = 0$, last $u_n = 24$). A WeeklySchedule holds a weekday, a Saturday and a Sunday/holiday profile. Time is counted in seconds from 00:00 of day 1, and start_weekday fixes the weekday of day 1.

A model step of length $Δt$ should receive the mean of the schedule over the step, not its value at one instant. schedule_mean integrates the step function exactly:

\[\bar v(t, t + Δt) = \frac{1}{Δt}\int_t^{t+Δt} v\,dt' ,\]

so a scheduled gain delivers exactly its scheduled energy for any step length. The daily integral is the equivalent full-load hours (full_load_hours).

DOE_MEDIUM_OFFICE_SCHEDULES is transcribed from the U.S. DOE commercial reference building Medium office, new construction, ASHRAE 90.1-2004 (Deru et al. 2011), EnergyPlus file RefBldgMediumOfficeNew2004_Chicago.idf: occupancy, lighting, plug loads, heating and cooling setpoints, HVAC operation. Full-load hours per week, summed by hand from the IDF and checked in the tests: occupancy 54.50 h, lighting 62.00 h, equipment 90.95 h.

2. Internal gains

InternalGains follows the EnergyPlus People, Lights and ElectricEquipment objects. At time $t$ (or averaged over a step):

sourcepowersplit
people$n\,f_{occ}\,M$sensible $S(M, T_{air})$, radiant fraction $f_r$ of it; latent $M - S$
lighting$P_L A\,f_{light}$radiant + visible → surfaces; return air → lost; rest → air
equipment$P_E A\,f_{equip}$radiant → surfaces; latent; lost; rest → air

The occupant sensible heat is the EnergyPlus correlation (occupant_sensible_heat),

\[S = C_0 + C_1 M + C_2 M^2 + T(C_3 + C_4 M + C_5 M^2) + T^2(C_6 + C_7 M + C_8 M^2),\]

with $T$ in °C, limited to $[0, M]$. It is a fit to Carrier's (1965) sensible gains at 350–1450 Btu h⁻¹ and 70–82 °F. The fit was constrained to $S = 0$ at 96 °F and $S = M$ at 30 °F. At those anchors the implementation gives $S/M ≤ 0.022$ and $|S - M|/M ≤ 0.004$ over the fitted range. At 120 W and 24 °C, $S = 69.5$ W. The coefficients come from the EnergyPlus source (InternalHeatGains.cc). The Engineering Reference prints two of them slightly differently ($C_0$, $C_5$); the difference is below 0.01 W.

The split conserves energy exactly: people + lighting + equipment = convective + radiative + latent + lost (InternalGainRates). ZoneForcing(r::InternalGainRates; …) passes the convective and radiative parts to the zone. Because the mix of people, lights and equipment changes over the day, the radiative fraction changes every step. ZoneForcing now accepts a per-step radiative_fraction that overrides the zone's.

A ThermostatSchedule holds heating and cooling setpoint schedules and an optional availability schedule. scheduled_hvac returns the IdealLoadsHVAC for time $t$; while the system is unavailable its capacities are zero and the zone floats.

The periodic-mean theorem

With the outdoor temperature constant and the occupant split frozen, the zone is a linear time-invariant system, $x_{n+1} = A x_n + B u_n$. Its periodic response to weekly gains has the same mean as its steady response to the week-mean gains. This holds exactly for the discrete θ-scheme too: averaging the recurrence over one period gives $\bar x = A \bar x + B \bar u$. The test drives the massive test box with the DOE office gains. The week-mean air temperature of the periodic response matches the steady temperature under the mean gains to 1.5 × 10⁻¹² K. A scheduled office week conserves the zone's heat to a relative 9 × 10⁻¹³.

3. Validation against ASHRAE Standard 140-2020

What was compared. The reference is the example results of six whole-building programs (BSIMAC, CSE, DeST, EnergyPlus, ESP-r, TRNSYS) in Informative Annex B8 of Standard 140-2020. The Standard's weather is Denver TMY3. These are program-to-program comparisons, not measurements. No measured data from an instrumented building was available, so the phase target "measured demand vs simulated" is not met by this validation. The Standard itself says the min–max ranges are not acceptance criteria. They are used here because they span what established tools compute for the same fully specified building.

Cases. The in-depth cases of §5.2.3 that have no transparent windows (window optics are issue #27):

Model inputs. The inputs follow the Standard's normative tables: materials, the 140-2020 surface coefficients, and infiltration as the Table 5-3 mass flow (1.0156 kg m⁻³ × 1015 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). The zone model lumps interior long-wave exchange into the surface-to-air coefficient, so it uses the Standard's interior combined coefficients (Table 5-9: walls 1.8, ceiling 1.7, floor 3.7, high-conductance element 4.5 W m⁻² K⁻¹). For the exterior, two options the Standard allows are compared:

  • explicit long-wave (FacetElement, the reported result): an UrbanFacet outer face with the Standard's convective coefficients (walls 11.9, roof 14.4, raised floor 0.8, element 8.0), emissivity 0.9, and long-wave irradiance $F_{sky} L_{sky} + (1 - F_{sky})σ T_{out}^4$. Here $L_{sky}$ is the weather file's horizontal infrared and the ground is at the outdoor air temperature (§5.2.1.8.2.2.1);
  • constant combined (EnvelopeElement): the Standard's combined exterior coefficients (21.6 / 21.8 / 5.2 / 17.8) with a sol-air source.

Plane-of-array irradiance comes from the Perez et al. (1990) model with ground reflectance 0.2, in the test driver validation/support/ashrae140_driver.jl. The zone is stepped six times per hour. Going to 12 steps per hour changes every annual heating load and every peak by less than 0.04 %, and the annual cooling loads by at most 0.12 % (Case 400, whose annual cooling is only 0.025 MWh). Each case gets a 31-day warm-up before the year.

Where it misses, and why.

The 11 misses among the other sensitivities are, in full: 250−220 annual heating and peak cooling, 430−420 peak cooling (solar); 400−395 annual and peak heating (high-conductance element); 800−430 annual cooling and both peaks (thermal mass); and 230−220 annual and peak cooling and 410−400 peak cooling (infiltration: +0.408 MWh vs 0.379–0.399, +0.731 kW vs 0.620–0.726, +0.259 kW vs 0.168–0.252 — the infiltration adds slightly too much cooling). The causes below are likely, not demonstrated.

  1. Solar-driven peaks. Case 250 peak cooling and the 430−420 and 250−220 peak differences are low, and so is the heating saved by the dark exterior (250−220: −1.476 MWh vs −1.805 … −1.556). Constant exterior convective coefficients represent the annual mean wind (4.02 m s⁻¹, per the Standard). The reference programs compute wind- and temperature-dependent coefficients, which are lower on calm, sunny afternoons.
  2. The high-conductance element. The 400−395 heating difference is 2.02 MWh against 1.34–1.90, so the element loses too much heat. The likely cause is the lumped interior: the element's long-wave exchange with the room surfaces goes through the air node.
  3. Thermal mass. The 800−430 reductions in cooling and in both peaks are weaker than the reference. The Standard's interior combined coefficient for walls (1.8 W m⁻² K⁻¹) couples the concrete block to the air weakly. The reference programs also couple it radiatively to the other surfaces.

With constant combined exterior coefficients only 28 of 72 checks fall inside the range. That variant misses the sky's long-wave deficit: the sky is on average about 16 K colder than the air. It therefore gives less heating in every case and more cooling in every low-absorptance case, and the tests assert this. The Standard's own Case 450 (constant coefficients) is likewise below Case 600 in heating, for all six reference programs (Table B8-9: −0.002 to −0.672 MWh).

4. An office cell with the DOE schedules (illustration)

This is an illustration, not a validation. It uses the Case 430 cell in the Denver weather, the DOE office gains (1 occupant per 18.58 m², 10.76 W m⁻² lighting and plugs, return-air fraction 0) and the same week-mean gain (532 W) in three runs:

gainsthermostatheating (MWh)sensible cooling (MWh)
constant at the week mean21 / 24 °C always4.722.29
DOE schedules21 / 24 °C always5.272.87
DOE schedulesDOE setpoints + HVAC availability2.742.55

The first two runs deliver the same energy as internal gains, but scheduling changes the demand. The daytime peak gain is partly removed by cooling, and the unoccupied nights and weekends then need heating (+12 % heating, +25 % cooling). This shows why a constant-gain building model misjudges demand. Setback and HVAC availability then halve the heating.
