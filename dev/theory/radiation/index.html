Radiative transfer

Radiation is the only term in the LES energy budget that is genuinely non-local: every other process — advection, diffusion, pressure — couples a cell to its neighbours, but a photon emitted at the surface can be absorbed ten kilometres above it, and a building can shade a street two hundred metres away. This page develops the theory behind Aeolus' radiation stack (src/RadiationCore.jl and the modules built on it) from the transfer equation down to the specific approximations each module makes, and states honestly where each approximation breaks.

1. The radiative transfer equation

Let $I_\nu(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\Omega})$ be the spectral radiance at position $\mathbf{x}$ travelling in direction $\hat{\Omega}$ — energy per unit time, per unit area normal to $\hat{\Omega}$, per unit solid angle, per unit wavenumber. Along a path parameterised by $s$, radiance is depleted by extinction and replenished by emission and by scattering from other directions:

\[\hat{\Omega} \cdot \nabla I_\nu = -\beta_{e,\nu} I_\nu + \beta_{a,\nu} B_\nu(T) + \frac{\beta_{s,\nu}}{4\pi} \int_{4\pi} p_\nu(\hat{\Omega}', \hat{\Omega})\, I_\nu(\hat{\Omega}')\, d\Omega' .\]

Here $\beta_a$, $\beta_s$ and $\beta_e = \beta_a + \beta_s$ are the absorption, scattering and extinction coefficients (units m⁻¹), $B_\nu(T)$ is the Planck function, and $p_\nu$ is the phase function, normalised so that $\frac{1}{4\pi}\int p \, d\Omega = 1$.

It is conventional to work in terms of three dimensionless quantities instead, and these are exactly the three fields carried by Aeolus' OpticalProperties type:

\[\tau = \int \beta_e \, ds, \qquad \omega = \frac{\beta_s}{\beta_e}, \qquad g = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{4\pi} p(\cos\Theta)\, \cos\Theta \, d\Omega .\]

$\tau$ is the optical depth (how opaque the layer is), $\omega$ the single-scattering albedo (what fraction of extinction is scattering rather than absorption — $\omega = 1$ is a purely scattering medium such as a cloud in the visible, $\omega = 0$ a purely absorbing one such as a gas line in the infrared), and $g$ the asymmetry parameter (the mean cosine of the scattering angle; $g = 0$ is isotropic or Rayleigh, $g \approx 0.85$ is forward-peaked scattering by cloud droplets, $g < 0$ is backscattering).

Combining optical properties

When several constituents — gas, cloud, aerosol — occupy the same layer, their optical depths add, but $\omega$ and $g$ must be combined as weighted means, because each is defined as a ratio. The correct rule, implemented in combine_optics!, is

\[\tau = \sum_i \tau_i, \qquad \omega = \frac{\sum_i \tau_i \omega_i}{\sum_i \tau_i}, \qquad g = \frac{\sum_i \tau_i \omega_i g_i}{\sum_i \tau_i \omega_i} .\]

$\omega$ is weighted by optical depth because it is a per-extinction ratio; $g$ is weighted by the scattering optical depth $\tau_i \omega_i$ because only scattered photons have a scattering angle at all. Getting this weighting wrong is a classic error that produces subtly wrong cloud albedos — a thin absorbing aerosol mixed with a thick scattering cloud will drag $g$ far too low if weighted by $\tau$ alone.

2. Gas optics: the spectral problem

The transfer equation above is monochromatic. Real gas absorption varies by six orders of magnitude between the centre of a rotational line and the window a fraction of a wavenumber away. Solving the transfer equation at line resolution — a line-by-line calculation — needs $\sim 10^6$ spectral points and is far too expensive inside an LES that calls radiation every few minutes.

Why band-averaging fails

The obvious shortcut — average the absorption coefficient over a spectral band and solve once — is wrong, and wrong in a specific direction. Transmission is exponential in $k$, and by Jensen's inequality

\[\overline{e^{-ku}} \;\ge\; e^{-\bar{k}u},\]

so the true band transmission always exceeds the transmission of the band-mean absorption coefficient. Physically: photons escape through the gaps between lines. Band-averaging systematically makes the atmosphere too opaque.

The correlated-k method

The correlated-k method fixes this by reordering. Within a band, the value of $k$ at a particular wavenumber does not matter for the band-integrated flux — only the distribution of $k$ values does. So define the cumulative distribution $g(k)$: the fraction of the band with absorption coefficient below $k$. Inverting gives a monotone $k(g)$ on $g \in [0,1]$, and

\[\overline{T} = \int_{\Delta\nu} e^{-k_\nu u}\, \frac{d\nu}{\Delta\nu} = \int_0^1 e^{-k(g) u} \, dg \approx \sum_{j} w_j \, e^{-k_j u}.\]

The last step is quadrature: a handful of g-points (Aeolus uses 2 per band by default, configurable) reproduces to a fraction of a percent what a million spectral points would give. The "correlated" assumption is that the ordering of $k$ values by wavenumber stays the same at different pressures and temperatures, so a given $g$ refers to the same spectral regions throughout the column. This is excellent within a band dominated by one species and degrades where bands overlap.

How Aeolus builds $k(g)$

Operational models (RRTMG, RRTMGP) tabulate $k(g, p, T)$ from line-by-line calculations and ship it as a data file. Aeolus does not (yet): when src/GasOptics.jl was written the NetCDF/HDF5 stack those files need did not load on the development machine, so it constructs the distribution analytically. Since #310 NCDatasets is a dependency and reading an RRTMGP table would be possible:

  1. A Malkmus statistical narrow-band model treats the band as a random array of Lorentz lines with an exponential-tailed inverse distribution of line strengths. Its band transmission has the closed form

    \[-\ln \overline{T} = \frac{\pi \gamma}{2\delta} \left[ \sqrt{1 + \frac{4 S u}{\pi \gamma}} - 1 \right],\]

    with $S/\delta$ the mean line strength per unit spacing and $\gamma$ the collision half-width — two parameters per species per band, which Aeolus embeds as tables.
  2. Domoto (1974) inversion recovers $k(g)$ from that transmission function by inverse Laplace transform, which for the Malkmus form is analytic and involves the error function.
  3. Pressure and temperature scaling is applied to the absorber path in the usual Curtis-Godson manner.

This is a reconstruction, not a fit to line-by-line data, and is correspondingly less accurate — but it is self-contained, it loads anywhere, and it lands the broadband fluxes within a few percent of observation (clear-sky OLR 229 W/m², surface downwelling 346 W/m², greenhouse effect 161 W/m²). An RRTMGP backend can be added later behind the same AbstractGasOptics interface wherever it loads.

Numerical hazard

At large absorber amounts $\overline{T}$ underflows to exactly zero in floating point, and $\ln \overline{T}$ becomes $-\infty$. Aeolus provides malkmus_log_transmission, which evaluates $\ln \overline{T}$ directly from the closed form above without ever forming $\overline{T}$. This is not a cosmetic fix — the unguarded version produces NaN optical depths in the lower troposphere.

3. The two-stream approximation

Even monochromatically, the transfer equation is an integro-differential equation in five dimensions. The two-stream approximation collapses the angular dimension by assuming the diffuse radiance field can be represented by just two hemispheric fluxes, $F^\uparrow$ and $F^\downarrow$. Substituting a two-term expansion into the transfer equation gives the coupled pair

\[\frac{dF^\uparrow}{d\tau} = \gamma_1 F^\uparrow - \gamma_2 F^\downarrow - \text{(source)}, \qquad \frac{dF^\downarrow}{d\tau} = \gamma_2 F^\uparrow - \gamma_1 F^\downarrow + \text{(source)} .\]

The different closures differ only in how $\gamma_1 \dots \gamma_4$ are defined. Aeolus ships three, all behind one interface:

ClosureBest forCharacter
Delta-Eddingtonshortwave, thick cloudtruncates the forward-scattering peak into the direct beam
PIFM (Zdunkowski)shortwave, generalpractical improved flux method; well-behaved at small $\omega$
QuadraturelongwaveGaussian angular quadrature; the standard LW choice

Delta-scaling

Cloud droplets scatter overwhelmingly forward: $g \approx 0.86$ means most "scattered" photons barely change direction. Treating them as scattered wastes the two-stream approximation's limited angular resolution. Delta-scaling removes a Dirac forward spike of weight $f = g^2$ and rescales the remainder:

\[\tau' = (1 - \omega f)\,\tau, \qquad \omega' = \frac{(1-f)\,\omega}{1 - \omega f}, \qquad g' = \frac{g - f}{1 - f}.\]

The scaled medium has a smaller optical depth and a much less forward-peaked phase function, and the two-stream solution of the scaled problem is far closer to the truth than that of the unscaled one.

The layer solution, and an overflow trap

For a homogeneous layer, the Meador & Weaver (1980) solution gives closed-form reflectance and transmittance in terms of $k = \sqrt{\gamma_1^2 - \gamma_2^2}$. As written in the paper, the expressions contain $e^{+k\tau}$ in both numerator and denominator. For $\tau = 10^4$ — an entirely reachable value in a correlated-k line centre — $e^{k\tau}$ overflows to Inf, and Inf/Inf is NaN.

Aeolus divides numerator and denominator through by $e^{k\tau}$ analytically, so every exponential that appears in the code is of the form $e^{-k\tau} \le 1$. The result is algebraically identical and numerically bounded at any optical depth.

Linear-in-$\tau$ longwave source

Within a layer the Planck source is not constant — the temperature varies across it, and in an optically thick layer the emission that escapes comes disproportionately from the near edge. Assuming a constant source therefore misplaces the emission. Writing $B(\tau) = B_{\text{top}} + \Delta \cdot \tau/\tau_d$ and integrating the layer emission properly, using

\[\int_0^x s\, e^{-s} \, ds = 1 - e^{-x}(1+x),\]

gives layer sources that are measurably more accurate: against a sub-layered reference flux of 180.66 W/m², the linear form errs by 0.55 W/m² where the constant form errs by 3.30 — a sixfold improvement at no extra cost.

Column solution: adding/doubling

Individual layer reflectances and transmittances are combined into a full column by the adding method, which accounts for the infinite series of internal reflections between two stacked layers in closed form:

\[R_{12} = R_1 + \frac{T_1 R_2 T_1}{1 - R_1 R_2}, \qquad T_{12} = \frac{T_1 T_2}{1 - R_1 R_2} .\]

The denominator $(1 - R_1 R_2)^{-1}$ is exactly the geometric sum of photons bouncing between the two interfaces — the same structure that reappears in §6 as the urban radiosity matrix inverse.

The adding recurrence is sequential in the vertical, but the g-points and the columns are independent. The multi-column solves (solve_sw! / solve_lw! on optics τ[k, g, column], #305) therefore run one work item per (g-point, column), with the recurrence over $k$ inside the item. The broadband flux is the sum over g-points, taken in ascending order. A single column is the one-column case of the same code, so it gets the same bits at every thread count.

4. From fluxes to heating rates

The quantity the LES actually needs is a temperature tendency. Net flux is defined positive downward, $F^{net} = F^\downarrow - F^\uparrow$, stored on levels (cell faces), and the heating rate of the layer between two levels follows from energy conservation:

\[\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\right)_{\text{rad}} = -\frac{1}{\rho c_p} \frac{\partial F^{net}}{\partial z} .\]

A layer that receives more flux than it passes on heats. Clear-sky tropospheric air cools radiatively at roughly 1–2 K/day — the measured Aeolus mean is −1.00 K/day, with the profile ranging from −2.48 to −0.089 K/day. This cooling is not incidental: it is the destabilising term that drives much of the convection an LES exists to resolve.

5. Solar geometry

The shortwave boundary condition requires the sun's position to sub-degree accuracy — an error of one degree in solar elevation near sunrise changes surface irradiance by tens of W/m². Aeolus implements the Meeus algorithm (src/SolarGeometry.jl): Julian day, geometric mean longitude and anomaly, equation of centre, apparent longitude with nutation, obliquity, declination and hour angle. Accuracy is better than 0.01° for present-day dates, against roughly 1° for the sinusoidal approximation the original radiation scaffold used.

Three corrections matter near the horizon, and all three are implemented:

  • Atmospheric refraction (Bennett's formula) lifts the apparent sun by about 34′ at the horizon — more than its own diameter, which is why the sun is already geometrically below the horizon at the moment it appears to set.
  • Air mass (Kasten & Young) departs sharply from $1/\cos\theta_z$ near grazing incidence; the secant formula diverges at the horizon while the true air mass is finite (≈ 38). Note that Kasten-Young is valid at the horizon, so the domain guard must be $h_{\text{apparent}} < 0$, not $\le 0$.
  • Earth-sun distance varies by ±1.7 % over the year, so extraterrestrial irradiance ranges from 1317 W/m² at aphelion to 1413 W/m² at perihelion about the 1361 W/m² mean.

Global irradiance is split into direct and diffuse components by the Erbs or Reindl correlations, keyed on the clearness index $k_t = \text{GHI}/\text{ETR}$. The split matters enormously in urban geometry: direct beam casts sharp shadows, diffuse does not, so the same total irradiance produces a completely different surface temperature pattern depending on how it is partitioned.

6. Beyond plane-parallel

Everything above assumes a plane-parallel atmosphere: horizontally infinite, uniform layers. That assumption is very good for the free troposphere and very bad for the two cases an urban LES cares most about — broken cloud fields, and street canyons.

Aeolus addresses each with a dedicated solver, documented in their own modules:

  • 3-D Monte-Carlo (src/MonteCarloRT.jl) traces individual photon packets through the voxel grid, capturing side illumination, shadow displacement and inter-cloud scattering that the independent-column approximation cannot represent. It is the reference solution against which the two-stream result is checked, not a production-speed solver. Every photon draws from its own counter-based random stream, keyed by the seed, the g-point and the photon index (#305). A solve is therefore reproducible from its seed and identical at every thread count, and there is no unseeded default.
  • Urban radiosity (src/Radiosity.jl) treats the built environment as a set of finite facets exchanging radiation through view factors, solving the multiple-reflection problem exactly rather than through a sky-view-factor approximation. Radiative trapping in canyons — where the effective albedo of a street falls well below the material albedo of its surfaces because photons get several chances to be absorbed — is a first-order driver of the urban heat island and cannot be captured plane-parallel.
  • Spectral surface optics (src/SurfaceOptics.jl) supplies the wavelength- and angle-dependent reflectance these solvers need, in place of a single grey albedo.
  • Aerosol optics (src/AerosolOptics.jl) supplies Mie-derived $\tau$, $\omega$ and $g$ for the aerosol burden, including hygroscopic growth, which dominates visibility and the direct radiative effect at high relative humidity.

3-D cloud shadows: the slant path

SolarGeometry.cloud_shadow_transmittance! gives the direct-beam transmittance at each surface cell by tracing the ray toward the sun through the 3-D cloud field. With $\tau_c$ the vertical optical depth of cell $c$ (extinction coefficient $\tau_c/\Delta z$) and $\ell_c$ the exact length of the ray inside it (a grid traversal, Amanatides & Woo 1987),

\[T_{ij} = \exp\Big(-\sum_{c} \frac{\tau_c}{\Delta z}\,\ell_c\Big),\]

which for a horizontally uniform layer is exactly $\exp(-\tau/\mu_0)$: the $1/\mu_0$ of the slant path is already in the path lengths. (Until #130 the march applied it a second time and returned $\exp(-\tau/\mu_0^2)$, 0.20 instead of 0.45 at 30° elevation for $\tau = 0.4$, and counted the lowest cell only half.)

The cloud optical depth is $\tau = k\,\rho q\,\Delta z$ with the mass extinction of RadiationCore, $k_{liq} = 1.5/r_{eff}$ m² g⁻¹ ($r_{eff}$ in μm; 0.15 m² g⁻¹ at 10 μm) and $k_{ice} = 1/r_{eff}$ — the same coefficients TwoStream.MieCloudOptics uses, so a column scheme and the shadow see the same cloud. A forward-peaked droplet phase function sends most of the light it "removes" on in almost the same direction; for the beam that reaches the ground the Berlin case therefore uses the delta-scaled depth $\tau' = (1 - \omega g^2)\tau$, the same scaling the two-stream solve applies ($\approx 0.26\,\tau$ for liquid in the visible).

An LES column under a standard atmosphere

An LES domain is a few hundred metres deep; its sky longwave and the sunlight at its top come from the ten kilometres above it. RadiationCore.column_from_profiles turns LES profiles into a hydrostatic column and stitch_columns puts it under the part of a deeper column (GasOptics.us_standard_column) above the LES top, with the pressure made continuous. A lower column that follows the standard column's own temperature and humidity gives the same surface $L^\downarrow$ (to 0.02 W/m²), outgoing longwave and global shortwave (to 0.12 W/m²) as the standard column alone.
