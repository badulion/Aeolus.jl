Fog and dew

This page covers the thermodynamic half of radiation fog in Aeolus (Phase 44, src/RadiationFog.jl): how much vapour condenses in the air or onto a surface, where the latent heat goes, and why the update cannot overshoot saturation. The droplet-radiative feedback that drives radiation fog — fog cooling itself by emitting longwave from its top — builds on these functions and is documented in Radiation fog.

1. What went wrong before

The urban example shipped an apply_fog_dew! that was switched off because "its warming term amplifies". Measured in a one-cell box model with the example's own settings (surface 280 K, air 288 K, $q_v$ = 10 g/kg held by resupply, $\Delta t$ = 2 s, rate $10^{-4}$ s⁻¹), it had two defects:

  1. Condensation in subsaturated air. When the surface was below the dew point, vapour was removed from the air and its latent heat added to the air, whatever the air's own humidity. Nothing tied the heating to air-side saturation, so there was no brake: the air warmed from 288 K to 363 K in 24 h while its relative humidity fell from 0.95 to 0.016.
  2. Explicit-rate overshoot. In supersaturated air it removed $r\,\Delta t\,(q_v - q_s)$ without the latent-heat feedback on $q_s$, overshooting below saturation for $r\,\Delta t \gtrsim 0.4$ ($q_v - q_s$ = −6.3×10⁻⁴ at $r\Delta t = 1$, −2.9×10⁻³ at 3).

In the full urban run (06–09 h, same settings) the first defect did not blow the run up within 3 h; it showed instead as the scheme draining the lowest layer of vapour entirely — $q_v$ = 0 in some cells after ~35 minutes — which is condensation from air at zero relative humidity.

2. The conserved quantity

For a reversible isobaric phase change the invariant is the moist enthalpy, referenced to liquid water (Emanuel 1994, ch. 4):

\[h = (c_{pd}\, q_d + c_l\, q_t)\, T + L_v(T)\, q_v - L_f(T)\, q_i ,\]

with the Kirchhoff latent heats of the contract. This is MoistThermodynamics.moist_enthalpy. The familiar moist static energy $c_{pm} T + L_v q_v$ is not invariant here: it double-counts the vapour heat capacity against $L_v(T)$ and changes by $(c_{pv} - c_l)\,T\,\Delta q$, about 24 % of $L_v \Delta q$ at 285 K.

Because $h$ is linear in $T$ with slope $c_{pm}$, condensing $\Delta q$ at fixed $h$ gives exactly

\[c_{pm}(q_\text{final})\, \Delta T = L_v(T_\text{initial})\, \Delta q ,\]

which the tests check to 10⁻¹⁰.

3. Fog: saturation-limited condensation

The target state solves $q_v - \Delta = q_s(T(\Delta), p)$ with $T(\Delta)$ from the enthalpy constraint. The residual is strictly decreasing in $\Delta$ — condensing removes vapour and warms the parcel, raising $q_s$ — and $\Delta \in [-q_l, q_v]$, so a safeguarded Newton iteration inside that bracket always converges (fog_equilibrium). Subsaturated air without fog is returned untouched after one saturation evaluation.

A finite condensation timescale $\tau$ relaxes toward that target by the exact exponential factor

\[\Delta q = \bigl(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\bigr)\, \Delta_\text{eq} ,\]

not by the explicit $\Delta t/\tau$, which exceeds 1 once $\Delta t > \tau$. Since the residual is monotone, any fraction in $[0, 1]$ leaves the parcel on the same side of saturation, for every $\Delta t$. The default $\tau = 0$ is full saturation adjustment: the phase-relaxation time of fog droplets at 100 cm⁻³ is a few seconds.

Relation to the contract's two saturation adjustments

The solver described above is not fog's own: it is the contract's process adjustment, MoistThermodynamics.isobaric_saturation_adjustment, and fog_equilibrium delegates to it with no rain or ice (issue #82). It was written here first and moved to the contract unchanged, so fog results are bit-for-bit the same; the tests assert === agreement over a sweep of states.

The contract also has saturation_adjustment, which is a different operation: a diagnosis that recovers $T$ and the partition from a prognostic $\theta_{li}$ and must reproduce $\theta_{li}$ exactly. The contract's $\theta_{li}$ is only approximately conserved by phase change — its moist Exner exponent $R_m/c_{pm}$ is taken at the current partition and changes as vapour condenses, where Bryan & Fritsch's exact form uses total water only — so applied to the same parcel the two disagree. At $p = p_\text{ref}$ they agree to $|\Delta q_l|/q_l \le 3.2\times10^{-3}$ and $|\Delta T| \le 10^{-3}$ K for 275–310 K and $S$ = 1.01–1.10, from the exponential $\theta_{li}$ form. At 800 hPa, 285 K and $S$ = 1.05 the diagnosis over-heats by 2.76 % of $L_v \Delta q$ and condenses 1.75 % less. Most of that gap is the partition-dependent exponent: frozen at total water, the diagnosis agrees with the enthalpy form to 0.2–0.3 % at every pressure (test/thermodynamics.jl). Condensation during a step is a process and uses the enthalpy form, which is exact; reading the state off a prognostic $\theta_{li}$ is a diagnosis and must invert whatever $\theta_{li}$ the model carries, approximation included. Before #82 the contract only had the diagnosis, and #85 recorded this gap as two @test_broken. They compared against the wrong operation, and are now passing tests against the process adjustment.

4. Dew: deposition with surface-side latent heat

Dew exchanges vapour between the lowest air layer (thickness $\Delta z$) and a surface at $T_s$ by the bulk law

\[\rho\, \Delta z\, \frac{dq_v}{dt} = -\rho\, C_e U\, \bigl(q_v - q_s(T_s)\bigr),\]

integrated exactly over the step with $T_s$ fixed. The air can at most be dried to $q_s(T_s)$, whatever $\Delta t$ is. The deposited mass $m = -\rho \Delta z \Delta q_v$ goes to a surface reservoir and its latent heat $L_v(T_s)\, m$ to the surface energy budget; the air temperature is unchanged, because the vapour that left carried its own sensible enthalpy. When the surface warms, dew re-evaporates, limited by the amount present.

With the urban example's coefficients ($C_e = 10^{-3}$, $U$ = 3 m/s) a surface 7 K below the dew point collects 0.049 mm/h, under Monteith's (1957) observed ceiling of about 0.07–0.08 mm/h.

Saturation is over liquid water. On a sub-freezing surface the deposit is really frost, which saturates over ice and releases $L_s$. That regime is not modelled; it is treated as supercooled dew.

5. In the urban example

The urban case's fog_dew coupling (src/components/urban/fogdew.jl) delegates to these functions (`fogadjust!, anddewexchangecolumns!for the dew on the first air cell of each column) and is switched on. Enabling it exposed that the example's moisture transport, forward-Euler with centered advection, is unconditionally unstable: with condensation off, relative humidity reached 2.07 in 8,395 cells within 1000 steps, which the 0.03 clamp had hidden. With condensation on, that noise turned into latent heat and the run blew up. Moisture (q_vand the fog waterq_l`, which must move with the air) now uses WENO-Z5 with the positivity limiter, after which the run stays bounded with fog and dew active.

Measured over the full 24 h run (06 h to 06 h, $\Delta t$ = 2 s): the lowest-layer $\theta$ stayed in 292.1–301.5 K and $\max|\nabla\cdot u|$ = 1.70×10⁻¹⁵ (re-measured after the fog_equilibrium convergence fix; 1784 s wall). Dew formed on the initially 285 K surfaces in the morning, peaked at 0.185 mm at 09:30 and had evaporated by 22:00. Fog water reached at most 3.4 g/kg, confined to wall nooks, and was gone by 15:30. The example's surfaces never cool below the dew point at night (the minimum $T_s$ rises from 285 K to 292 K over the day), so it contains no nocturnal radiation fog. Two known residuals come from the example's IBM velocity correction, which is applied after the projection and is not divergence-free in cells next to buildings. First, a few wall-nook cells gain vapour: fog now caps them at saturation. Second, the lowest-layer $q_v$ drops to 0 in some cells between 18:30 and 22:30, while neither dew nor fog is active.

A third residual belonged to the example's surface coupling, not to this module. The example's bulk evaporation apply_latent_flux! ($\beta$-limited) and dew re-evaporation were computed independently. On a surface that still carried dew and was above the dew point, the two evaporation rates therefore added, reaching up to $(1+\beta)$ times the potential rate. Physically a dew-wetted surface evaporates at the potential rate ($\beta = 1$). The excess lasted only while dew remained, and at most 0.185 mm was present in the run. Since #129 the Berlin case has one vapour exchange per surface: over soil and water the Monin–Obukhov evaporation of the surface model (negative when the air is above saturation at the skin, which puts the dew into the soil water), and the dew reservoir of this module only over surfaces with no moisture of their own (roofs and glass), exchanging with the first air cell above them.
