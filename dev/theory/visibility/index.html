Visibility

Visibility is the diagnostic that transport, aviation and air-quality users read first, and it is almost entirely an optics problem: given the particles in a volume of air, how far can an observer see? This page develops the diagnostic in src/Visibility.jl (Phase 44), from Koschmieder's law to the extinction of fog, haze and clear air, and states where each approximation breaks.

1. Koschmieder's law

An observer looks at a black object against the horizon sky through a homogeneous atmosphere with extinction coefficient $\beta_{ext}$ (m⁻¹). Light from the object is attenuated as $e^{-\beta x}$; airlight scattered into the line of sight adds a brightness that approaches the horizon-sky brightness as $1-e^{-\beta x}$. The apparent contrast against the sky therefore decays as

\[C(x) = C_0\,e^{-\beta_{ext} x},\qquad C_0 = -1 \text{ for a black object}.\]

Visibility is the distance at which $|C|$ falls to the observer's contrast threshold $\varepsilon$ (Koschmieder 1924):

\[V = \frac{-\ln\varepsilon}{\beta_{ext}}.\]

Two thresholds are in use:

threshold$-\ln\varepsilon$name
$\varepsilon = 0.02$3.912Koschmieder's value; assumed by the Kunkel (1984) fit. Default (KOSCHMIEDER_THRESHOLD).
$\varepsilon = 0.05$2.996WMO meteorological optical range, MOR (MOR_THRESHOLD).

The MOR is shorter by $\ln 20/\ln 50 = 0.766$. Every visibility quoted by this module states its $\varepsilon$.

Koschmieder assumes a uniform path, a black target, a uniform horizon sky and daylight. It does not describe night-time visibility of lights (Allard's law), nor slant visibility through a fog top.

2. Extinction

Extinction coefficients of independent scatterers add, so

\[\beta_{ext} = \beta_{drop} + \beta_{haze} + \beta_{Rayleigh}\]

evaluated at 550 nm, the peak of photopic sensitivity.

2.1 Droplets

For a droplet number distribution $n(r)$ (m⁻⁴),

\[\beta_{drop} = \int_0^\infty \pi r^2\,Q_{ext}\!\left(\frac{2\pi r}{\lambda}, m_w\right) n(r)\,dr.\]

$Q_{ext}$ is the Mie extinction efficiency from Phase 37 (AerosolOptics.mie_sphere) with the Hale & Querry refractive index of water, $m_w = 1.333 + 2\times10^{-9} i$ at 550 nm. For large drops $Q_{ext}\to 2$ — the extinction paradox: a large sphere removes twice its geometric cross-section, half by absorption and reflection and half by diffraction into a narrow forward lobe. The approach is slow; the Nussenzveig & Wiscombe (1980) edge term

\[Q_{ext} \simeq 2 + 1.9923861\,x^{-2/3}\]

still leaves $Q_{ext}-2 = 1.3\times10^{-3}$ for a 5 mm raindrop ($x = 5.7\times10^4$). The module uses Mie up to $x = 10^4$ and this asymptote above it. Pointwise, Mie oscillates about the asymptote by up to $3\times10^{-3}$ (interference ripple); averaged over ±10 % in $x$ the two agree to 0.03 % ($x = 10^3$) – 0.14 % ($x = 9\times10^3$).

Three spectra are provided: monodisperse, the Hansen (1971) gamma distribution

\[n(r) \propto r^{(1-3b)/b}\,e^{-r/(ab)},\qquad a = r_{eff},\; b = v_{eff},\]

and a binned spectrum (also built from a Phase 41 superdroplet ensemble). For the gamma the shape factor $k = (r_v/r_{eff})^3 = (1-b)(1-2b)$ is exact, so a spectrum built from a bulk $(\mathrm{LWC}, N_d)$ pair takes its effective radius from AerosolCloud.droplet_effective_radius with that $k$ — the same droplet geometry the Phase 42 indirect-effect diagnostics use. $v_{eff} = 0.1$ gives $k = 0.72$, inside the Martin et al. (1994) range.

In the geometric limit $Q_{ext}=2$,

\[\beta_{drop} = 2\pi N\langle r^2\rangle = \frac{3\,\mathrm{LWC}}{2\rho_w r_{eff}} \;\propto\; N_d^{1/3}\,\mathrm{LWC}^{2/3},\]

which is exactly the per-metre form of the cloud optical depth $\tau = 3\,\mathrm{LWP}/(2\rho_w r_{eff})$ used by TwoStream and AerosolCloud; the test suite checks the two agree to $10^{-12}$. At fixed water, more droplets mean lower visibility — the aerosol indirect effect acting on fog. For fog spectra ($r_{eff}$ = 2–15 μm) Mie is 3–14 % above the geometric limit.

Quadrature. Water barely absorbs at 550 nm, so $Q_{ext}(x)$ carries narrow resonance spikes. The gamma integral uses Simpson in $\ln r$ with at least 10 points per unit of size parameter; against a 60 000-interval reference the error is typically $3\times10^{-4}$ and at worst $1.9\times10^{-3}$ ($r_{eff}=2$ μm). A coarser 5-points-per-π rule, as used for the aerosol modes in Phase 37, aliased the resonances into errors of up to $5\times10^{-3}$ for droplets and was rejected. The quadrature returns $\beta = \pi N\langle r^2\rangle\,\bar Q$: it integrates only the area-weighted mean efficiency $\bar Q$ and takes $\langle r^2\rangle$ analytically. Normalising by the numerically integrated number instead (the first implementation) let the small-drop number cut off at the lower integration bound — most of $N$ when $v_{eff}\to 0.5$ — inflate $\beta$, to an effective $Q_{ext} = 6.6$ at $v_{eff} = 0.49$; the area-weighted form gives 2.12.

2.2 Haze

Unactivated aerosol grows hygroscopically as the humidity rises and dominates visibility between clear air and fog. This term is Phase 37's AerosolOptics.bulk_optics: κ-Köhler wet radius and Mie for each log-normal mode of an OPAC mixture. Measured at 550 nm, with Rayleigh included, $\varepsilon = 0.02$:

mixtureRH 0 %50 %80 %95 %
continental clean170 km151 km112 km50 km
urban (RH 80 %)12.0 km

2.3 Clear air

Molecular scattering sets the upper bound. With $\beta_R = n\,\sigma_R(\lambda)$, $n = p/(k_B T)$ and $\sigma_R$ from GasOptics.rayleigh_cross_section (the same cross-section the radiation code uses), 1013.25 hPa, 288.15 K and 550 nm give $\beta_R = 1.27\times10^{-5}$ m⁻¹ and $V = 307$ km. That cross-section is a pure $\lambda^{-4}$ fit about 10 % above the Bucholtz (1995) / Bodhaine et al. (1999) tables, so the true Rayleigh-limited visibility is closer to 340 km; the bias is inherited, not duplicated.

3. Empirical fog parameterisations

Operational models diagnose fog visibility from bulk water alone:

  • Kunkel (1984): $\beta = 144.7\,\mathrm{LWC}^{0.88}$ km⁻¹ (LWC in g m⁻³), i.e. $V = 27\,\mathrm{m}\times\mathrm{LWC}^{-0.88}$ at $\varepsilon = 0.02$. No droplet-number dependence.
  • Gultepe et al. (2006): $V = 1.002\,\mathrm{km}/(\mathrm{LWC}\,N_d)^{0.6473}$ (LWC in g m⁻³, $N_d$ in cm⁻³), fitted to FRAM-C observations.

Comparison with the explicit calculation (gamma, $v_{eff}=0.1$, $N_d = 100$ cm⁻³, Mie, 550 nm):

Koschmieder/Kunkel ranges 0.83–1.07 and Koschmieder/Gultepe 0.75–0.76. Dense fog of 0.1–0.3 g m⁻³ gives visibilities of order 100 m, an order of magnitude below the 1 km fog definition. The residual differences are what one expects: Kunkel carries no $N_d$ dependence, and Gultepe was fitted to observed (often broader, bimodal) spectra and to measured visibility rather than an ideal black target.
