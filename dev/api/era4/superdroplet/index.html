Superdroplet microphysics (Phase 41)

Superdroplet is the Lagrangian probabilistic reference of Era IV (Shima et al. 2009). The droplet population is carried as computational particles, each with a multiplicity — the number of real droplets it stands for — plus the wet radius and dissolved solute mass those droplets share. Collision–coalescence is a Monte Carlo process over $\lfloor N/2 \rfloor$ random non-overlapping pairs with the multiple-coalescence rule, so it costs $O(N)$ and conserves water mass to round-off; condensation is the diffusional growth equation solved per particle. The size distribution is never assumed: it is measured from the ensemble, which is what makes this the scheme a bulk closure (P3, the TurbulentMicrophysics moment closure) is judged against. The Golovin-kernel test in test/era4_superdroplet.jl checks the multiplicity bookkeeping against the analytic solution.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.AerosolProperties — Type
AerosolProperties{T}

A soluble aerosol species, described by the Petters & Kreidenweis (2007) hygroscopicity parameter κ and its dry density ρ_s.

κ collapses molecular weight, density, van 't Hoff factor and osmotic coefficient into one number calibrated against measured growth factors: κ ≈ 1.28 for NaCl, 0.61 for ammonium sulfate, ~0.1 for aged organics, 0 for an insoluble (wettable) particle. It is used here instead of the raw van 't Hoff bundle because it is what measurements actually constrain.

Construct one from van 't Hoff quantities with aerosol_from_vant_hoff if that is the form you have.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.CollisionKernel — Type
CollisionKernel

Abstract supertype. A kernel is called as collision_kernel(K, r1, r2) and returns the coalescence rate coefficient in m³ s⁻¹: the volume of air swept per unit time per unit number density, so that the expected number of collisions between two droplet classes of concentrations n₁ and n₂ in volume ΔV per unit time is K n₁ n₂ ΔV.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.EulerianCell — Type
EulerianCell{T}

The host grid cell a superdroplet population exchanges water and heat with. A fixed-mass, fixed-volume parcel: density and volume are constant, so the air mass ρ ΔV is an exact invariant and specific humidities are unambiguous.

That choice is what makes the coupling testable. With a fixed air mass, q_vap + q_liq is conserved to round-off by construction — condensation moves mass between two numbers with the same denominator. If the cell were held at fixed pressure instead, ρ would change with T and the "conservation" law would acquire a correction term that is easy to get wrong and impossible to distinguish from a real bug.

Pressure is therefore diagnostic: cell_pressure returns ρ R_m T.

Fields

  • temperature (K), q_vap (kg kg⁻¹ of moist air),
  • density (kg m⁻³, moist air, fixed), volume (m³, fixed).
Aeolus.Superdroplet.GolovinKernel — Type
GolovinKernel(b)

The additive (Golovin 1963) kernel K = b (V₁ + V₂) with V the droplet volume and b in s⁻¹.

It is not a physical kernel. It is the validation kernel, because the Smoluchowski coagulation equation with an additive kernel and an exponential initial spectrum has a closed-form solution in a modified Bessel function I₁ — see golovin_mass_density. Any Monte Carlo coalescence solver that cannot reproduce it is wrong, and the failure modes (dropped multiplicity, missing N(N-1)/2 scaling, wrong γ rule) all show up as visible distortions of the mass spectrum rather than as subtle biases.

Literature runs quote b in the mass form K = b_m (m₁ + m₂) with b_m = 1500 cm³ g⁻¹ s⁻¹ = 1.5 m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹; the volume-form coefficient is b = b_m ρ_w = 1500 s⁻¹. Use GolovinKernel(b_mass = 1.5) to pass it in the mass form directly.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.GravitationalKernel — Type
GravitationalKernel(m = DEFAULT_MICRO)

The bare gravitational kernel, π (r₁+r₂)² |Δv| with unit collision efficiency — HydrodynamicKernel with UnitEfficiency. Kept as its own type because it is the standard upper bound quoted for collection rates, and comparing it against the Long-efficiency kernel is the clearest way to see how much of the collection bottleneck is hydrodynamic rather than kinematic.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.HydrodynamicKernel — Type
HydrodynamicKernel(efficiency = LongEfficiency(), m = DEFAULT_MICRO)

The hydrodynamic (gravitational-sweepout) kernel

K(r₁, r₂) = π (r₁ + r₂)² E(r₁, r₂) |v(r₁) - v(r₂)|

— the geometric cross-section of the pair, times the differential fall speed, times the probability E that a droplet on a collision course is actually collected rather than swept around the collector.

Note the structural consequence of the |Δv| factor: equal-sized droplets never collide under this kernel. Real clouds get around that through turbulence and through the size spread that condensation leaves behind, and the fact that a purely gravitational kernel gives exactly zero for a monodisperse population is the cleanest statement of why warm rain needs something to break the symmetry.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.LongEfficiency — Type
LongEfficiency()

Long's (1974) analytic fit to the Hall/Davis-Sartor collision efficiencies:

E(R, r) = min(1, 4.5e8 R² (1 - 3e-8/r))     for R < 50 μm
E(R, r) = 1                                 for R ≥ 50 μm

with R the larger and r the smaller radius, both in metres (the coefficients are Long's cgs 4.5e4 cm⁻² and 3e-6 cm converted).

Two things this captures and a constant efficiency does not. First the steep R² growth: collection is hopeless for 10 μm droplets (E ~ 4e-3) and easy by 50 μm (E → 1), which is precisely the "condensation-coalescence bottleneck" that makes warm-rain initiation hard. Second the 1 - 3e-8/r factor, which cuts off collection of very small droplets — they follow the streamlines around the collector.

Substituting this into π(R+r)² E |Δv| with Stokes fall speeds reproduces Long's polynomial collection kernel, which is why LongKernel and HydrodynamicKernel(LongEfficiency()) agree in the small-drop limit.

The tabulated Hall (1980) efficiencies are more accurate, particularly in the 20-40 μm "efficiency gap"; they are a 15×21 measured table and are deliberately not reproduced from memory here.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.LongKernel — Type
LongKernel()

Long's (1974) polynomial collection kernel, in the form used by the box-model literature (Bott 1998; Shima et al. 2009):

K = 9.44e9 (V₁² + V₂²)      if max(r₁,r₂) ≤ 50 μm     [m⁻³ s⁻¹ · m⁶]
K = 5.78e3 (V₁ + V₂)        otherwise                  [s⁻¹ · m³]

with V in m³. The first branch is the small-drop asymptotic of π(R+r)² E |Δv| with Long's efficiency and Stokes fall speeds — the R² of the efficiency, the R² of the cross-section and the R² of the Stokes velocity difference multiply up to V². The second branch is the large-collector limit, where E → 1 and the kernel becomes additive again.

Cheap, smooth, and much closer to reality than Golovin; used here as the "physically plausible" kernel in tests that do not need an analytic answer.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.MicrophysicalConstants — Type
MicrophysicalConstants{T}

Material properties of water, air and the vapour/heat diffusion pair that the droplet-scale physics needs and that MoistThermodynamics — which is a bulk thermodynamics contract — does not carry.

Nothing thermodynamic is duplicated here: L_v, e_s, R_v and the moist heat capacity all come from MoistThermodynamics. What lives here is the stuff that only appears once you resolve individual drops: surface tension (Kelvin term), liquid density, dynamic viscosity and mean free path of air (terminal velocity), and the diffusivities of vapour and heat.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.SuperdropletSet — Type
SuperdropletSet{T}

The computational-particle ensemble, stored struct-of-arrays: one Vector per attribute rather than one Vector of structs.

That layout is not a micro-optimisation here, it is the natural one. The two hot loops touch disjoint attribute sets — collision-coalescence reads ξ and radius only, condensation reads radius, solute_mass and writes radius — so an AoS layout would drag the position vectors through cache on every collision sweep for nothing. It also makes the compact! operation (removing multiplicity-zero particles) a simple per-array pass.

Fields

  • ξ::Vector{Int} — multiplicity: the number of real droplets this computational particle represents. Integer on purpose; the whole exactness of the coalescence bookkeeping rests on it.
  • radius::Vector{T} — wet radius (m).
  • solute_mass::Vector{T} — dissolved solute mass (kg).
  • x, y, z::Vector{T} — position (m).
  • u, v, w::Vector{T} — velocity (m s⁻¹); w is the air velocity, sedimentation is added separately at advection time.
  • cell::Vector{Int} — host Eulerian cell index (1-based; 0 = unassigned).
  • aerosol::AerosolProperties{T} — the solute species (one per set).
Aeolus.Superdroplet.SuperdropletSet — Method
SuperdropletSet(T = Float64; aerosol = NACL, n = 0)

An empty set (or one with n placeholder particles) for solute species aerosol.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.UnitEfficiency — Type
UnitEfficiency()

E ≡ 1: every drop swept out geometrically is collected. The idealisation behind the bare "gravitational" kernel. It overestimates collection badly for small droplets, where the collected drop is carried around the collector by the flow.

Aeolus.MoistThermodynamics.effective_radius — Method
effective_radius(sd; cell = nothing) -> m

r_eff = Σξr³ / Σξr² — the ratio of the volume to the area moment.

This, not the mean radius, is what radiative transfer sees: the extinction of a droplet population depends on its total cross-section and its water content, and r_eff is exactly the size of the monodisperse population with the same two. For a typical cloud spectrum it exceeds the mean radius by 10-25 %, and using the mean radius instead biases cloud albedo the wrong way by several per cent.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.advance_positions! — Method
advance_positions!(sd, dt; velocity = nothing, sedimentation = true,
                   domain = nothing, m = DEFAULT_MICRO)

Advect the superdroplets for dt with forward Euler.

velocity(x,y,z) -> (u,v,w) supplies the air velocity (the stored u,v,w are used if it is nothing); with sedimentation = true each droplet additionally falls at its own terminal_velocity, which is the only place droplet size feeds back on transport and hence the mechanism by which rain separates from cloud.

domain = ((x0,x1),(y0,y1),(z0,z1)) wraps periodically in x and y and clamps in z.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.aerosol_from_vant_hoff — Function
aerosol_from_vant_hoff(i, M_s, ρ_s, name; M_w = 0.018015268)

Convert the classical van 't Hoff description of a solute (dissociation number i, molar mass M_s, density ρ_s) into a κ value, using κ = i ρ_s M_w / (ρ_w M_s) — the dilute-limit identification of the two formulations (Petters & Kreidenweis 2007, eq. 8).

For NaCl (i=2, M_s=0.05844, ρ_s=2160) this gives κ = 1.33, close to the measured 1.28; the 4 % gap is the non-ideality the measured κ absorbs.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.apply_condensate_tendency! — Function
apply_condensate_tendency!(cell, Δm, q_liq = 0, c = DEFAULT_CONSTANTS)
    -> (Δq_vap, ΔT)

Push a condensed water mass Δm (kg, positive = condensation) back onto the host cell:

Δq_vap = -Δm / m_air,
ΔT     = +L_v(T) Δm / (m_air c_pm).

Both halves of the coupling in one place, deliberately: vapour removal and latent heating must use the same Δm and the same L_v(T), or the cell's moist static energy drifts. L_v and c_pm both come from MoistThermodynamics, so they are consistent with the e_s used to compute the supersaturation that drove the growth in the first place.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.assign_cells! — Method
assign_cells!(sd, origin, spacing, dims) -> sd

Map every superdroplet position onto a 1-based linear index of a regular Cartesian cell grid with lower corner origin = (x0,y0,z0), cell size spacing = (dx,dy,dz) and dims = (nx,ny,nz) cells, using column-major ordering (i + nx(j-1) + nx·ny(k-1)).

This is the Lagrangian→Eulerian half of the coupling: it is what decides which droplets are allowed to collide with which, and which host cell receives their latent heat. Positions outside the box are clamped into the nearest cell rather than dropped, so no water is silently lost.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.bessel_i1 — Method
bessel_i1(z) -> I₁(z)

Modified Bessel function of the first kind, order 1, written out here because SpecialFunctions is a binary dependency that does not build in this environment — and because this is the function the headline validation rests on, so it had better be independently verifiable.

Two representations:

  • Series, I₁(z) = (z/2) Σ (z²/4)^k / (k!(k+1)!), for |z| ≤ 50. All terms are positive, so there is no cancellation and the relative accuracy is at the level of the accumulated round-off, ~1e-15.
  • Asymptotic, I₁(z) ~ e^z/√(2πz) [1 - 3/(8z) - 15/(128z²) - …], beyond. At z = 50 the two agree to 5e-11, which is the crossover accuracy.

Verified in the test suite against tabulated I₁(0.5), I₁(1), I₁(2), I₁(5), I₁(10).

Aeolus.Superdroplet.bessel_i1_scaled — Method
bessel_i1_scaled(z) -> e^{-z} I₁(z)

The exponentially scaled I₁. This is the form the Golovin solution actually needs: at late times its argument reaches several hundred, where I₁ itself overflows Float64 while the product with the accompanying exp(-(1+T)x̃) is a perfectly ordinary number. Combining the exponents analytically — the Golovin exponent becomes -x̃(1-√T)², which is never positive — is the difference between a solution that evaluates and one that returns NaN exactly where the interesting drizzle tail lives.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.coalescence_probability — Method
coalescence_probability(K, r_j, r_k, ξ_max, dt, ΔV, scale) -> p

The scaled pair coalescence probability of the superdroplet method,

p = scale · max(ξ_j, ξ_k) · K(r_j, r_k) · Δt / ΔV.

Three factors, each with a job:

  • max(ξ_j, ξ_k) — the pair (j,k) stands for ξ_j ξ_k real droplet pairs, but a single "all-or-nothing" event is allowed to consume at most min(ξ_j, ξ_k) of them, so the per-event probability carries the larger multiplicity and the number of events per attempt carries the smaller. This is the substitution that makes the method O(N) instead of O(N²) without biasing the mean.
  • K Δt / ΔV — the ordinary Smoluchowski probability that two given droplets in a well-mixed volume ΔV collide in Δt.
  • scale = [N(N-1)/2] / ⌊N/2⌋ — the compensation for sampling only ⌊N/2⌋ non-overlapping pairs out of the N(N-1)/2 that exist.

p is routinely larger than 1 in the rain regime, which is exactly why the caller must use the γ (multiple-coalescence) rule and not a coin flip.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.coalescence_step! — Method
coalescence_step!(rng, sd, kernel, dt, volume;
                  indices = eachindex(sd.ξ), m = DEFAULT_MICRO)
    -> (ncollisions, nevents)

One Shima all-or-nothing collision-coalescence sweep over the superdroplets listed in indices, assumed to share a well-mixed air volume.

Algorithm

  1. Randomly permute the N candidates and pair them up as (1,2), (3,4), … — L = ⌊N/2⌋ non-overlapping pairs. Every superdroplet takes part in at most one candidate collision per step, which is what keeps the bookkeeping consistent when multiplicities change mid-sweep.

  2. Scale each probability by N(N-1)/2 / L (see coalescence_probability).

  3. Draw the number of coalescences for the pair: γ = ⌊p⌋ + (rand() < p - ⌊p⌋ ? 1 : 0), then cap it at ⌊ξ_j/ξ_k⌋ with ξ_j ≥ ξ_k — one event can consume at most as many droplets as the smaller-multiplicity particle has partners for.

  4. Apply the multiplicity update:

    • ξ_j - γ ξ_k > 0 (the generic case): ξ_k of the j-droplets are consumed γ at a time by the k-droplets. So ξ_j ← ξ_j - γ ξ_k, ξ_k unchanged, and the k attributes grow: r_k ← (r_k³ + γ r_j³)^{1/3}, m_{s,k} ← m_{s,k} + γ m_{s,j}.
    • ξ_j - γ ξ_k = 0 (exhaustion): all j-droplets are consumed. The pair is then split into two identical grown droplets sharing ξ_k: ξ_j ← ⌊ξ_k/2⌋, ξ_k ← ξ_k - ⌊ξ_k/2⌋, both taking the grown attributes. Splitting rather than deleting is what keeps N_sd from collapsing over a long run.

Conservation

Water mass is conserved identically in both branches, not to within a tolerance: (ξ_j - γξ_k) r_j³ + ξ_k (r_k³ + γ r_j³) = ξ_j r_j³ + ξ_k r_k³, and in the exhaustion branch (⌊ξ_k/2⌋ + ⌈ξ_k/2⌉)(r_k³ + γ r_j³) = ξ_j r_j³ + ξ_k r_k³ because ξ_j = γ ξ_k there. Only the cube-root/cube round-trip on the radius costs anything, and that is one ulp.

The real droplet number drops by exactly γ ξ_k per event in both branches.

Returns (ncollisions, nevents): the number of real coalescences Σ γ ξ_k and the number of pairs that coalesced at all.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.collision_coalescence! — Method
collision_coalescence!(rng, sd, kernel, dt, cell_volumes;
                       ncells = length(cell_volumes), sweep = true)
    -> (ncollisions, nevents)

Multi-cell driver: bucket the superdroplets by sd.cell and run one coalescence_step! per cell with that cell's air volume.

Collisions are strictly intra-cell. That is the standard superdroplet assumption and it is a real approximation: it says the cell is well mixed on the timescale of dt, so a droplet is equally likely to meet any other droplet in the cell and none outside it. It converges as the cell shrinks only if the superdroplet count per cell stays adequate, which is the usual practical constraint (~30-100 per cell).

With sweep = true zero-multiplicity particles are removed afterwards.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.compact! — Method
compact!(sd) -> sd

Remove every particle whose multiplicity has fallen to zero.

Zero-multiplicity particles are produced by the coalescence rule when a pair with equal multiplicities merges and one half of the split rounds to zero. They are harmless (they represent no real water) but they cost work and they dilute the effective N_sd, so they are swept periodically rather than every step.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.condensation_growth! — Method
condensation_growth!(sd, S, T, p, dt; substeps = 0, mc, c) -> Δm

Grow every superdroplet at a prescribed ambient state and return the total real water mass condensed (kg; negative for net evaporation). Does not touch the host cell — see condensation_step! for the coupled version.

S is either one supersaturation for every droplet or a vector indexed like sd.radius, giving each superdroplet the supersaturation of the sub-grid environment it sits in (Phase 45 uses this to let droplets sample a sub-grid supersaturation distribution). A vector filled with a single value gives bit-for-bit the scalar result.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.condensation_step! — Method
condensation_step!(sd, cell, dt; index = nothing, nsub = 0,
                   substeps = 0, mc, c) -> (Δm, Δq_vap, ΔT)

Two-way coupled condensation: grow the droplets in the ambient supersaturation, remove the condensed vapour from the cell and deposit the latent heat there, both consistently.

The outer loop is sub-stepped because the coupling itself is stiff: the phase relaxation time

τ_phase ≈ [4π D_v Σ(ξ r) / ΔV]⁻¹

is a few seconds in a typical cloud and a fraction of a second in a polluted one. A single Euler step longer than τ_phase drives S past zero and back, and the droplets alternately grow and evaporate. With nsub = 0 the count is chosen from τ_phase itself; the inner grow_droplet then handles the per-droplet stiffness on top.

Returns the condensed mass (kg) and the cell tendencies actually applied.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.deposit_liquid_water! — Method
deposit_liquid_water!(qc, sd, cells; mc = DEFAULT_MICRO) -> qc

Deposit the superdroplet liquid water onto an Eulerian array qc (specific liquid water content, kg kg⁻¹), one entry per cell, given the matching vector of EulerianCells. qc is zeroed first.

Nearest-cell ("cloud in cell") deposition rather than a smoothing kernel: it conserves mass exactly and introduces no length scale that is not already in the grid. A trilinear deposition would be smoother but would leak mass across the boundary of the domain unless carefully closed.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.diffusional_growth_rate — Function
diffusional_growth_rate(r, m_s, S, T, p, aerosol, mc, c) -> dr/dt, m s⁻¹

The droplet diffusional growth equation with curvature and solute terms (Rogers & Yau 1989, eq. 7.17; Pruppacher & Klett 1997 §13.1.2):

r dr/dt = (S - a/r + b/r³) / (F_k + F_d)

S is the ambient supersaturation (e/e_s - 1), a/r the Kelvin term and b/r³ the Raoult term; F_k and F_d are the heat- and vapour-diffusion resistances.

The a, b terms are not decoration. Without them there is no activation barrier, every particle grows monotonically from its dry size and the cloud droplet number becomes whatever the aerosol number is. With them, a particle below its critical supersaturation settles at a haze equilibrium and one above it runs away — and which particles do which emerges from the ambient S(t) history rather than from a parameterisation.

The 1/r prefactor is also why the equation is stiff: a 0.1 μm haze particle relaxes to equilibrium in microseconds while a 10 μm droplet takes minutes, so an explicit step sized for the cloud droplets will send the haze to negative radius. See grow_droplet.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.droplet_critical_radius — Function
droplet_critical_radius(m_s, T, a, mc, c) -> m

Köhler critical radius r_c = √(3b/a), the top of the activation barrier. For a 1e-16 kg NaCl particle at 283 K it is about 1.0 μm — roughly 50 times the dry radius.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.droplet_critical_supersaturation — Function
droplet_critical_supersaturation(m_s, T, a, mc, c) -> S_c

Köhler critical supersaturation S_c = √(4a³/27b), the height of the activation barrier. Scales as m_s^(-1/2): a ten-fold heavier nucleus activates at a ~3.2 times lower supersaturation, which is why the largest aerosol always activate first and why the activated fraction is so sensitive to the updraught that sets peak S.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.droplet_equilibrium_radius — Function
droplet_equilibrium_radius(m_s, S, T, a, mc, c) -> m

The stable (haze) equilibrium radius of an unactivated particle at ambient supersaturation S < S_c: the smaller of the two roots of S_eq(r) = S, found by bisection on [r_d, r_c] where S_eq is monotonically increasing.

Returns NaN if S ≥ S_c, because then there is no equilibrium — the particle activates.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.droplet_kohler_supersaturation — Function
droplet_kohler_supersaturation(r, m_s, T, a, mc, c) -> S_eq

Equilibrium supersaturation over a solution droplet, in the classical two-term Köhler form

S_eq(r) = a/r - b/r³

with a the Kelvin coefficient and b = κ r_d³ the Raoult coefficient.

The curve rises from strongly negative at haze sizes (the solute holds water at sub-saturation), peaks at the critical radius r_c = √(3b/a) with value the critical supersaturation S_c = √(4a³/27b), and decays as a/r beyond. A droplet held at ambient S > S_c is past the barrier and grows without bound — it is activated; one at S < S_c settles onto the stable haze branch. Nothing imposes this: it is a consequence of the shape of the curve, and it is why this module can let activation emerge rather than parameterising it.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.fd_vapor — Function
fd_vapor(T, p, m = DEFAULT_MICRO, c = DEFAULT_CONSTANTS) -> s m⁻²

The vapour-diffusion resistance,

F_d = ρ_w R_v T / (D_v e_s(T)),

using e_s from MoistThermodynamics.saturation_vapor_pressure so that the growth equation and the host model's saturation agree exactly.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.fk_thermal — Function
fk_thermal(T, m = DEFAULT_MICRO, c = DEFAULT_CONSTANTS) -> s m⁻²

The thermodynamic (latent-heat) resistance to diffusional growth,

F_k = (L_v/(R_v T) - 1) · L_v ρ_w / (K_a T).

Growing a droplet releases latent heat that must be conducted away; until it is, the droplet surface is warmer than the environment and its own saturation vapour pressure is higher, which throttles the growth. F_k and F_d are comparable in magnitude near 273 K — the thermal term is not a correction, it halves the growth rate.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.golovin_T — Method
golovin_T(b, L, t) -> T

The dimensionless time of the Golovin solution, T = 1 - exp(-b L t), where b is the mass-form additive kernel coefficient (m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹) and L the liquid water content (kg m⁻³).

T runs from 0 to 1 over the whole evolution: it is the fraction of the initial droplet population that has been consumed by coalescence, since N(t) = N₀(1-T) exactly for this kernel. T = 0.5 is "half the droplets gone", T = 0.99 a fully developed drizzle spectrum.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.golovin_mass_density — Method
golovin_mass_density(r, t, n0, r̄, b; ρ_water) -> g(ln r), kg m⁻³

The Golovin solution expressed the way coalescence results are actually plotted: mass per unit volume of air per unit ln r.

dM = x n(x) dx and x ∝ r³ ⟹ dx = 3x d(ln r), hence

g(ln r) = 3 x² n(x, t)

with x = (4π/3) ρ_w r³. This is the curve mass_distribution is compared against.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.golovin_number_density — Method
golovin_number_density(x, t, n0, x̄, b) -> n(x,t), m⁻³ kg⁻¹

The analytic solution of the Smoluchowski coagulation equation for the additive kernel K(x,y) = b(x+y) with the exponential initial condition n(x,0) = (n₀/x̄) e^{-x/x̄} (Golovin 1963; Scott 1968).

With x̃ = x/x̄ and T = 1 - e^{-b n₀ x̄ t},

n(x,t) = (n₀/x̄) · (1-T)/(x̃ √T) · exp[-(1+T) x̃] · I₁(2 x̃ √T)

Evaluated here in the numerically safe grouping

n = (n₀/x̄) · (1-T)/(x̃ √T) · exp[-x̃(1-√T)²] · [e^{-2x̃√T} I₁(2x̃√T)]

whose exponent is never positive, so it is finite everywhere.

Why this is the right validation

It is a genuine external check. The solution was derived from the continuous coagulation equation with no reference to any numerical method, it is nontrivial (a Bessel function, not an exponential), and every way of getting the superdroplet bookkeeping wrong distorts it in a visible and different way — dropping the N(N-1)/2 scaling slows the whole evolution, mishandling γ for p > 1 truncates the large-drop tail, splitting multiplicities wrongly breaks mass conservation. Two exact properties make it self-checking as well: ∫n dx = n₀(1-T) and ∫x n dx = n₀x̄ for every T, both of which the test suite verifies by quadrature before comparing anything to the Monte Carlo.

The T → 0 limit reduces to the initial exponential, since I₁(z) → z/2.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.grow_droplet — Function
grow_droplet(r0, m_s, S, T, p, dt, aerosol, mc, c;
             substeps = 0, rtol = 1e-8) -> r

Integrate the diffusional growth equation for one droplet over dt at fixed ambient S, T, p.

Why implicit, and in r²

Two things make the naive r ← r + dt·(dr/dt) explode. First, the equation is written in r dr/dt, so it is natural in w = r²: dw/dt = 2 g(√w) removes the 1/r singularity from the discretisation even though it is still there in g. Second, the haze branch is a stable equilibrium approached on a microsecond timescale: an explicit step of a second overshoots it by orders of magnitude and alternates sign. Backward Euler on w,

w^{n+1} - w^n - 2 Δt g(√(w^{n+1})) = 0,

is unconditionally stable on that branch and lands on the equilibrium instead of ringing about it.

The residual is solved with safeguarded Newton: a bracket [r_dry, r_big] is maintained (the residual is negative at r_dry, where the solute term dominates and forces growth, and positive at large r, where the S-bounded growth cannot keep up with w itself), a Newton step is taken when it stays inside, and the bracket is bisected when it does not. That cannot diverge, which matters because this is called once per droplet per substep.

substeps = 0 (the default) picks the substep count adaptively from the droplet's own relaxation time — activated droplets get one step, haze particles get as many as they need.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.init_exponential_superdroplets — Method
init_exponential_superdroplets(rng, N_sd, n0, r_mean, volume;
                               aerosol = NACL, solute_mass = 0,
                               stratified = false, T = Float64)

Sample N_sd superdroplets from the exponential mass distribution

n(x) dx = (n₀/x̄) exp(-x/x̄) dx,    x̄ = (4π/3) ρ_w r_mean³

at number concentration n₀ (m⁻³) in a cell of volume (m³), using constant multiplicity ξ = round(n₀ · volume / N_sd).

This is Shima et al.'s (2009) initialisation and it is the initial condition for which Golovin's analytic coalescence solution is written, so it is what the headline validation needs.

Constant multiplicity rather than constant mass-per-particle: sampling the number distribution and giving every computational particle the same weight is unbiased for number-weighted quantities and is what makes the Monte Carlo error scale as N_sd^(-1/2).

With stratified = true the uniform deviates are drawn one per equal- probability stratum instead of independently. This removes the leading sampling noise (it is a quasi-Monte-Carlo variance reduction, not a change of distribution) and is useful when one wants the deterministic part of the coalescence error rather than the sampling part; the convergence study uses stratified = false so that the N^(-1/2) law is actually visible.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.kappa_kohler_supersaturation — Function
kappa_kohler_supersaturation(r, m_s, T, a, mc, c) -> S_eq

The exact κ-Köhler equilibrium supersaturation (Petters & Kreidenweis 2007, eq. 6):

S_eq(r) + 1 = (r³ - r_d³) / (r³ - r_d³(1-κ)) · exp(a/r)

valid down to r → r_d, where it correctly returns -1 (a completely dry particle has zero water activity) instead of the classical form's divergence. Used as the reference the two-term form is checked against.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.kelvin_a — Function
kelvin_a(T, m = DEFAULT_MICRO, c = DEFAULT_CONSTANTS) -> m

The Kelvin (curvature) coefficient a = 2σ / (ρ_w R_v T), so that the equilibrium supersaturation over a pure-water drop of radius r is a/r. About 1.2e-9 m at 273 K — i.e. a 1 μm pure drop needs 0.12 % supersaturation just to stay alive, and a 10 nm one needs 12 %.

That divergence as r → 0 is exactly why cloud droplets cannot nucleate homogeneously in the atmosphere and need aerosol.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.mass_distribution — Method

Binned mass distribution g(ln r) = dM/d(ln r) in kg m⁻³ per unit ln r, the standard presentation of a coalescence calculation (Berry & Reinhardt 1974; Shima et al. 2009 Fig. 2).

Mass rather than number because coalescence is a mass cascade: the number density falls monotonically and hides everything, while g(ln r) shows the cloud mode draining into a drizzle mode as a second hump — which is the feature the analytic Golovin solution predicts and the feature a Monte Carlo solver with broken multiplicity bookkeeping gets visibly wrong.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.raoult_b — Method

The solute (Raoult) coefficient b = κ r_d³, so that the solute lowering of equilibrium supersaturation is -b/r³.

b = κ r_d³ is the dilute limit of the κ-Köhler expression; see kappa_kohler_supersaturation for the exact form and the test suite for the demonstration that they agree to better than 1 % once r ≳ 3 r_d, which covers everything from haze upward.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.relative_dispersion — Method
relative_dispersion(sd; cell = nothing) -> (σ_r, ε)

Standard deviation of the number-weighted radius distribution and the relative dispersion ε = σ_r / r̄.

ε is the single number that summarises spectral width, and it is the parameter bulk schemes must assume. Observed warm clouds sit at ε ≈ 0.2-0.5; pure condensational growth drives it down (see the narrowing test), and it is collision-coalescence and entrainment mixing that push it back up. A scheme that gets ε wrong gets autoconversion wrong by a large factor, because autoconversion depends on the tail.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.stokes_settling_velocity — Function
stokes_settling_velocity(r, ρ_p, m = DEFAULT_MICRO) -> m s⁻¹

Stokes settling speed of a rigid sphere of radius r (m) and density ρ_p (kg m⁻³) with the Cunningham slip correction 1 + 2.51 λ/d:

\[v = \left(1 + 2.51\frac{λ}{d}\right)\frac{(ρ_p - ρ_a)\, g\, d^2}{18 μ}\]

This is regime 1 of terminal_velocity, which calls it with ρ_p = ρ_water; it is exposed separately so that dry aerosol (dust, biological ice-nucleating particles in IceNucleation) settles with the same drag law and air properties as cloud droplets. Valid for particle Reynolds number ≪ 1, i.e. d ≲ 20 μm for water and ≲ 15 μm for mineral dust (Seinfeld & Pandis 2016, ch. 9).

Aeolus.Superdroplet.surface_tension — Function
surface_tension(T, m = DEFAULT_MICRO) -> N m⁻¹

Surface tension of the water/air interface, σ = σ₀ + (dσ/dT)(T - T₀) with T₀ = 273.15 K. Gives 0.0728 N m⁻¹ at 20 °C, the textbook value.

The temperature dependence is not cosmetic: σ enters the Kelvin term linearly, and the Kelvin term is what sets the critical supersaturation, so a 10 K error here moves activation thresholds by ~1 %.

Aeolus.Superdroplet.terminal_velocity — Function
terminal_velocity(r, m = DEFAULT_MICRO) -> m s⁻¹

Still-air terminal fall speed of a water drop of radius r.

Three regimes, as the physics demands — the drag law genuinely changes character twice between a 1 μm droplet and a 5 mm raindrop:

  1. d < 19.5 μm — Stokes flow with the Cunningham slip correction 1 + 2.51 λ/d. v ∝ r²; the slip term matters below ~2 μm where the drop is no longer large compared with the mean free path of air.

  2. 19.5 μm ≤ d ≤ 1.07 mm — Beard's (1976) regime-2 fit: a 6th-order polynomial in ln N_Da where N_Da = 4 ρ_a Δρ g d³ / (3 μ²) is the Davies (Best) number, giving the Reynolds number and hence v. This covers the whole cloud-to-drizzle range and reproduces the measured 4.0 m s⁻¹ for a 1 mm drop.

  3. d > 1.07 mm — the Atlas et al. (1973) fit v = 9.65 - 10.3 exp(-600 d) (m, m s⁻¹), which captures the flattening toward the ~9.2 m s⁻¹ ceiling that drop deformation imposes. Beard's own regime 3 needs a physical-property number and a second polynomial; the Atlas fit is used here instead because it is short enough to state exactly. The two fits differ by 6 % at the junction, so they are blended linearly over 1.07–1.4 mm to keep v(r) continuous — a discontinuity here would show up directly as a kink in the gravitational collision kernel.

The drop is assumed spherical, so the fit is not extended past 7 mm, where real drops break up anyway.
