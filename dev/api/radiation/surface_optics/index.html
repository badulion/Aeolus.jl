Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.SNOW_SOOT_CONSTANT — Constant

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.AbstractBRDF — Type
AbstractBRDF

A bidirectional reflectance distribution model. Concrete subtypes implement

brdf(model, θ_i, φ_i, θ_v, φ_v) -> BRF

returning the bidirectional reflectance factor BRF = π f_r, with all angles in radians, θ measured from the zenith, and both directions pointing away from the surface (towards the sun and towards the sensor respectively).

Every model shipped here is reciprocal: brdf(m, θi, φi, θv, φv) == brdf(m, θv, φv, θi, φi). Reciprocity is a consequence of time-reversal symmetry of the microscopic optics and is not optional — a non-reciprocal BRDF makes the surface a source or a sink of radiation depending on which way the light happens to be travelling, and the test suite checks it for every model and preset.

Integrated quantities (black_sky_albedo, white_sky_albedo, blue_sky_albedo) are obtained by numerical integration of brdf and therefore work for any subtype without further code.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.BRDFSurface — Type
BRDFSurface{T,B} <: AbstractSurfaceOptics

Adapter that turns an AbstractBRDF — which describes angular structure and carries a single broadband brightness — into a full surface optics object with per-band albedos.

The construction separates the two: the BRDF supplies the shape (and thus the ratio of black-sky to white-sky albedo at every sun angle), while a material's spectral curve supplies the per-band brightness. Band b gets

\[α_{dir}(b, μ_0) = σ_b \, α_{bs}(μ_0), \qquad α_{dif}(b) = σ_b \, α_{ws},\]

with σ_b the ratio of the material's band albedo to its solar-weighted broadband albedo, so that the broadband albedo of the composite is exactly the BRDF's own. Setting material = nothing gives a spectrally flat surface.

            emissivity = 0.95, lw_spec = longwave_bands())

Wrap model as surface optics on shortwave grid spec. If material is a symbol from material_names, its spectral curve sets the band-to-band variation; emissivity may be a scalar, a material symbol or a per-longwave-band vector.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.CookTorranceBRDF — Type
CookTorranceBRDF{T}(ρd, F0, α)

Microfacet (Cook-Torrance 1982) BRDF for specular surfaces — glazing, sheet metal, wet asphalt — in the modern GGX formulation:

\[f_r = \frac{ρ_d}{π} + \frac{D(θ_h)\,G(θ_i, θ_v)\,F(c_d)}{4\cosθ_i\cosθ_v}\]

with

\[D(θ_h) = \frac{α^2}{π\bigl[\cos^2θ_h(α^2-1)+1\bigr]^2}, \qquad G = G_1(θ_i)\,G_1(θ_v), \qquad G_1(θ) = \frac{2\cosθ}{\cosθ + \sqrt{α^2 + (1-α^2)\cos^2θ}},\]

\[F(c_d) = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - c_d)^5\]

where h is the half vector between the incident and view directions, θ_h its zenith angle, and c_d = s·h = v·h.

The three ingredients each fix a specific failure of a naive specular model:

  • D, the Trowbridge-Reitz/GGX normal distribution, gives the microfacet slopes. Its long tails — decaying as θ_h⁻⁴ rather than the Gaussian exp(-θ_h²/α²) of the original Beckmann form — are why real glossy surfaces have a bright halo around the specular highlight, and why GGX replaced Beckmann in every renderer.
  • G, the Smith shadowing-masking term, removes the energy that would be reflected by microfacets which are hidden behind their neighbours. Without it the model gains energy at grazing incidence.
  • F, Schlick's approximation to the Fresnel coefficient, takes the reflectance from its normal-incidence value F0 to unity at grazing. This is what makes a window opaque and mirror-like when the sun is low, and it is the reason a glazed façade can throw more radiation at the street than the sky does.

α is the RMS microfacet slope: α ≈ 0.02 for float glass, 0.1-0.2 for painted or weathered metal, 0.2-0.3 for a wet road. F0 follows from the refractive index, F0 = ((n-1)/(n+1))² — 0.043 for glass (n = 1.52), 0.021 for a water film (n = 1.34) — or is measured directly for metals, where it is large and coloured.

Reciprocity holds exactly: D, G and the prefactor are symmetric under i ↔ v, and s·h = v·h by construction.

Reference: Cook & Torrance (1982), ACM Trans. Graph. 1, 7-24; Walter et al. (2007) for the GGX distribution and its Smith term.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.FresnelWater — Type
FresnelWater{T} <: AbstractSurfaceOptics

Wind-roughened water surface: Cox-Munk wave-slope statistics, unpolarised Fresnel reflection off each facet, plus whitecap and water-leaving contributions.

Fields: wind_speed (m s⁻¹ at 10 m), n_water (real refractive index), whitecap_reflectance, the per-band water_leaving reflectance and the longwave emissivity.

The albedo of the direct beam is obtained by integrating over the facet slope distribution rather than over the view hemisphere:

\[α_F(θ_0) = \frac{\displaystyle\int\!\!\int p(z_x, z_y)\, R_F(θ_{loc})\,\frac{\max(0,\, \hat{s}\cdot\hat{n})} {\cos θ_n}\,dz_x\,dz_y} {\displaystyle\int\!\!\int p(z_x, z_y)\, \frac{\max(0,\, \hat{s}\cdot\hat{n})}{\cos θ_n}\, dz_x\,dz_y}\]

where p is the Gaussian slope density, n̂ = (-z_x, -z_y, 1)/√(1+z_x²+z_y²) is the facet normal, cos θ_loc = ŝ·n̂ the local incidence angle, and the 1/cos θ_n converts horizontal area to true facet area. The weighting by the projected area ŝ·n̂ is what makes wind reduce the grazing-angle albedo: the facets that collect the beam are the ones tilted towards the sun, and those have the smallest local incidence angle.

Writing it as a normalised average (rather than dividing by cos θ_0) guarantees α_F ≤ max R_F ≤ 1 for any wind speed, and reduces exactly to R_F(θ_0) in the flat-water limit. Facet shadowing at extreme grazing incidence is neglected, which is the main approximation here; it becomes significant only beyond ~85°, where the direct beam carries almost no energy anyway.

             whitecap_reflectance = 0.22, emissivity = 0.96,
             lw_spec = longwave_bands())

Construct a wind-roughened water surface on shortwave grid spec.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.LambertianBRDF — Type
LambertianBRDF{T}(ρ)

Isotropic reflector: f_r = ρ/π in every direction, so BRF = ρ.

This is the reference case, and it exists mainly to be a test oracle. Its black-sky albedo is ρ at every solar zenith angle and its white-sky albedo is ρ; any quadrature that fails to reproduce those to ~1e-10 is broken, and no result obtained with it can be trusted for the other kernels.

Real surfaces are never Lambertian. Even fresh snow, the closest natural approximation, shows a 10-20% forward-scattering enhancement at low sun.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.RPVBRDF — Type
RPVBRDF{T}(ρ0, k, Θ)

Rahman-Pinty-Verstraete (1993) semi-empirical BRDF:

\[BRF(θ_i, θ_v, Δφ) = ρ_0 \, \bigl[\cos θ_i \cos θ_v (\cos θ_i + \cos θ_v)\bigr]^{k-1} \, F(g) \, \bigl[1 + R(G)\bigr]\]

with

\[F(g) = \frac{1 - Θ^2}{\bigl(1 + Θ^2 + 2Θ\cos g\bigr)^{3/2}}, \qquad R(G) = \frac{1 - ρ_0}{1 + G}, \qquad G = \sqrt{\tan^2 θ_i + \tan^2 θ_v - 2\tanθ_i\tanθ_v\cos Δφ}\]

The three parameters separate the three things a reflectance field actually does:

  • ρ0 sets the overall brightness (roughly the nadir reflectance at nadir illumination);
  • k ∈ (0, 1] controls the bowl shape — the Minnaert exponent. For k < 1 reflectance rises towards the limb, which is what a rough, porous surface does; k = 1 removes the anisotropy of the Minnaert term entirely;
  • Θ ∈ (-1, 1) is the Henyey-Greenstein asymmetry. Θ < 0 is backward-scattering (the normal case for vegetation and soil: the scatterers hide their own shadows), Θ > 0 forward-scattering (snow, wet surfaces).

R(G) is the hot-spot factor. G is the distance between the incident and view directions projected onto the surface plane, so G = 0 exactly at backscatter, where the factor reaches its maximum 2 - ρ0, and decays to 1 as the directions separate. Physically this is the shadow-hiding opposition effect: looking down the illumination direction you see no shadows, because every shadow is hidden behind the object that casts it. It is a genuinely sharp feature — the width of 1/(1+G) is set by nothing but geometry — and it is the single largest source of error in BRDF models that omit it.

RPV is not intrinsically energy-conserving, and for k < 1 the Minnaert factor diverges as θ_i → π/2, so the black-sky albedo grows without bound at the horizon. The presets shipped in rpv_preset all satisfy α_bs ≤ 1 out to 85° incidence, which is checked by the test suite; do not rely on the model beyond that.

Reference: Rahman, Pinty & Verstraete (1993), J. Geophys. Res. 98, 20791-20801.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.RossLiBRDF — Type
RossLiBRDF{T}(f_iso, f_vol, f_geo; br = 1, hb = 2, clamp_negative = true)

The MODIS operational kernel-driven BRDF — a linear combination of an isotropic term, the Ross-thick volume kernel and the Li-sparse-reciprocal geometric kernel:

\[BRF = f_{iso} + f_{vol}\,K_{vol}(θ_i, θ_v, Δφ) + f_{geo}\,K_{geo}(θ_i, θ_v, Δφ)\]

This is the model behind MCD43, the global albedo product, and its appeal is that it is linear in the unknowns: three cloud-free observations at different geometries determine (f_iso, f_vol, f_geo) by least squares, and the integrated albedos then follow from the kernel integrals, which are pure numbers independent of the scene.

Both kernels carry sec θ, so the model diverges at the horizon and its black-sky albedo is not bounded by unity for θ_i beyond about 87°; the shipped presets are verified to conserve energy out to 85°. MODIS itself limits retrievals to solar zenith angles below 70° for this reason.

Because the kernels are not individually non-negative (K_geo in particular is strongly negative in the forward-scattering direction), the linear combination can extrapolate below zero at extreme geometries. With clamp_negative = true (the default) the returned factor is floored at zero, which is physical — a surface cannot reflect negatively — and preserves reciprocity, since max(0, ·) of a symmetric function is symmetric. Set it to false to recover the raw linear model, which is what the published kernel-integral constants refer to.

References: Wanner, Li & Strahler (1995), J. Geophys. Res. 100, 21077; Lucht, Schaaf & Strahler (2000), IEEE TGRS 38, 977.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.SnowSurface — Type
SnowSurface{T} <: AbstractSurfaceOptics

Snow with grain-size, ageing and impurity dependence.

Fields: grain_radius (the fresh grain radius, μm), age_days, soot_ppmw (black carbon mass mixing ratio in parts per million by weight), the derived effective_radius, the per-band diffuse albedo and the longwave emissivity.

Snow is also the surface with the strongest solar-zenith dependence that still behaves itself: the direct-beam albedo follows the asymptotic escape function,

\[α_{dir}(μ_0) = α_{dif}^{\,u(μ_0)}, \qquad u(μ_0) = \tfrac{3}{7}\,(1 + 2μ_0)\]

which has two properties worth stating. It is bounded in [0,1] for any μ_0, unlike the linear-in-1/μ₀ parameterisations that overshoot unity for bright snow at low sun; and 2∫₀¹ u(μ)μ dμ = 1, so the hemispherically averaged exponent is exactly 1 and the direct albedo integrates back to the diffuse albedo, as the definition of the white-sky albedo requires.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.SnowSurface — Method

            soot_ppmw = 0, growth_rate = SNOW_GROWTH_RATE,
            emissivity = 0.99, lw_spec = longwave_bands())

Build a snow surface. The reference spectrum is the tabulated :snow_fresh curve at 50 μm; grain growth, ageing and soot are applied on top of it band by band.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.SpectralAlbedo — Type
SpectralAlbedo{T} <: AbstractSurfaceOptics

Per-band direct and diffuse shortwave albedo plus per-band longwave emissivity for a single homogeneous material.

Fields:

  • name — material symbol
  • spec — the shortwave SpectralGrid the albedos live on
  • lw_spec — the longwave SpectralGrid the emissivities live on
  • direct — black-sky (directional-hemispherical) albedo per shortwave band at overhead sun, μ₀ = 1
  • diffuse — white-sky (bihemispherical) albedo per shortwave band
  • emissivity — emissivity per longwave band
  • zenith_d — Briegleb steepness parameter for the solar-zenith dependence of the direct-beam albedo

direct and diffuse are genuinely different quantities, not two copies of the same table: diffuse is the integral of the material's measured reflectance curve over the band, and direct is that value evaluated at overhead sun through the normalised Briegleb shape, where it is typically 15-20% lower for a vegetated surface. Query the albedo at an arbitrary sun angle with surface_albedo(s, band, μ₀).

One caveat for very bright bands. The Briegleb shape multiplies the diffuse albedo by up to (1+d)/N(d) at grazing incidence, which for a band albedo already near unity — pure snow in the visible is 0.99 — would push the result above 1. surface_albedo clamps, and the clamp costs a few parts per thousand of the exact hemispheric consistency between the direct and diffuse albedos. For snow use SnowSurface instead: its escape-function form α_dir = α_dif^u(μ₀) is bounded in [0,1] by construction and needs no clamp.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.TiledSurface — Type
TiledSurface{T} <: AbstractSurfaceOptics

An area-weighted mosaic of surfaces — the thing an urban grid cell actually is. A 100 m cell in a European city is perhaps 35% roof, 25% road and pavement, 25% garden and 15% tree canopy, and each of those reflects and emits differently.

\[α(b, μ_0) = \sum_i f_i\,α_i(b, μ_0), \qquad ε(b) = \sum_i f_i\,ε_i(b), \qquad \sum_i f_i = 1\]

Linear area weighting is exact for the reflected flux provided the tiles do not exchange radiation with each other. In a street canyon they emphatically do — that is what Phase 35's radiosity solver is for — so TiledSurface is the right model for a flat mosaic (a field with patches of bare soil and grass, a flat roofscape) and the correct input to the radiosity solver for a three-dimensional one, not a replacement for it.

Components may be any AbstractSurfaceOptics, including another TiledSurface, so mosaics nest.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.TiledSurface — Method
TiledSurface(components, fractions)

Build a mosaic from a vector of surface-optics objects and their fractional areas. The fractions must be non-negative and sum to 1 to within 1e-8; silently renormalising them would hide the most common cause of a wrong urban energy balance, which is a land-cover table that does not close.

Aeolus.RadiationCore.material_albedo — Method
material_albedo(name, spec = shortwave_bands(); lw_spec = longwave_bands())

Build a SpectralAlbedo for material name on the shortwave grid spec. Band albedos are obtained by integrating the material's reflectance curve against the solar spectrum over each band — change the band structure and the numbers follow — and the emissivities by integrating the emissivity curve against the terrestrial Planck function over each longwave band.

sw = shortwave_bands()
α  = material_albedo(:grass, sw)
surface_albedo(α, 10, 0.5)   # visible band, 60° sun
surface_albedo(α,  8, 0.5)   # near-infrared: ~6× larger — the red edge

Available materials: asphalt, concrete, brick, rooftilered, rooftilegrey, glass, metalroof, soildry, soilwet, sand, grass, treedeciduous, treeconiferous, water, snowfresh, snow_old.

Aeolus.RadiationCore.surface_albedo — Method
surface_albedo(w::FresnelWater, band, μ₀) -> α

Total direct-beam albedo of the water surface in shortwave band:

\[α = (1 - W)\,\bigl[α_F(θ_0) + ρ_{wl}(b)\bigr] + W\,ρ_{wc}\]

combining the Cox-Munk Fresnel term, the band's water-leaving (subsurface backscatter) reflectance and the whitecap fraction W. At overhead sun in the visible this gives ≈ 0.04, of which about half is water-leaving — which is why clear ocean looks blue rather than mirror-grey from above, and why the near-infrared bands, where the water-leaving term vanishes, are the ones used to detect the sun glint.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics._band_mean — Method
_band_mean(anchors, ν_lo, ν_hi, T_weight; ngl = 6) -> Real

Planck-weighted band mean of a piecewise-linear spectrum:

\[\bar{ρ} = \frac{\int_{ν_{lo}}^{ν_{hi}} ρ(λ(ν))\,B_ν(T_w)\,dν} {\int_{ν_{lo}}^{ν_{hi}} B_ν(T_w)\,dν}\]

The integral is split at every anchor wavelength that falls inside the band, so each sub-interval has a smooth integrand (linear × Planck) and a 6-point Gauss-Legendre rule is essentially exact. Integrating straight through the kinks — which is what a single quadrature over the whole band would do — costs two to three digits on materials with sharp features such as the vegetation red edge.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics._phase_cosine — Method
_phase_cosine(θi, θv, Δφ) -> cos g

Cosine of the phase angle g, the angle between the view direction and the incident direction, both pointing away from the surface:

\[\cos g = \cos θ_i \cos θ_v + \sin θ_i \sin θ_v \cos Δφ\]

g = 0 is exact backscatter (the hot spot); g = π would be exact forward transmission. Note that the scattering angle ξ, measured between the photon's incoming and outgoing propagation directions, satisfies cos ξ = -cos g: backscatter is ξ = 180°. Both the RPV phase function and the Ross-thick kernel are conventionally written in terms of g, and that is the convention used here.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.black_sky_albedo — Method
black_sky_albedo(model, θ_i; kwargs...) -> α

Black-sky (directional-hemispherical) albedo: the fraction of a collimated beam arriving at zenith angle θ_i that the surface reflects back into the upward hemisphere.

Computed by numerical integration of the model's own BRDF, so it is consistent with brdf by construction and not by assertion. For a LambertianBRDF of albedo ρ the result is ρ at every angle, to quadrature precision — this is the calibration test for everything else in this section.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.black_sky_albedo — Method
black_sky_albedo(m::CookTorranceBRDF, θ_i; nψ = 64, nφ = 128) -> α

Specialised quadrature for the microfacet model, obtained by changing the integration variable from the outgoing direction v to the half vector h (Jacobian dΩ_v = 4 (v·h) dΩ_h) and then stretching the half-vector polar angle by the roughness, u = tan θ_h / α, u = tan ψ:

\[α_{bs}(θ_i) = ρ_d + \frac{1}{π\cosθ_i} \int_0^{2π}\!\!\int_0^{π/2} \sinψ\cosψ\sqrt{1 + α^2\tan^2ψ}\; G\,F\,(s\!\cdot\!h)\; \mathbb{1}[\cosθ_v > 0]\;dψ\,dφ_h\]

This is the same integral, exactly transformed — no approximation is introduced. What it buys is conditioning: in the original variables the integrand is a spike of angular width α sitting at a location that moves with θ_i, so a uniform hemisphere rule needs O(1/α) nodes per dimension and silently under-estimates the albedo when it does not have them. After the transformation the peak is pinned at ψ of order unity and 64 × 128 nodes suffice for α down to 0.01. The test suite checks the two paths against each other at a roughness where the brute-force rule is still trustworthy.

The sanity check the transformation must pass: for a perfect mirror (α → 0, F0 = 1) the integrand reduces to sin ψ cos ψ / π and the double integral gives exactly 1.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.blue_sky_albedo — Method
blue_sky_albedo(model, θ_i, f_diffuse; kwargs...) -> α

The albedo the surface actually presents to the atmosphere:

\[α_{blue}(θ_i, f) = (1 - f)\,α_{bs}(θ_i) + f\,α_{ws}\]

f_diffuse is the diffuse fraction of the incoming shortwave — near 0.1 for a clear sky with a high sun, 1.0 under thick cloud. The linear combination is exact to the extent that the diffuse sky is isotropic; under a clear sky it is not (it is strongly peaked around the sun and towards the horizon), which is the leading error in this formulation and is worth a percent or two of the albedo at low sun.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.broadband_albedo — Function
broadband_albedo(s, μ₀ = nothing) -> α

Solar-weighted broadband albedo — direct-beam at μ₀, or white-sky if μ₀ is nothing:

\[\bar{α} = \frac{\sum_b S_b\,α_b}{\sum_b S_b}\]

with S_b the top-of-atmosphere solar irradiance in band b taken from the spectral grid. This is the single number that is usually quoted for a surface, and it is derived here rather than tabulated, so it is guaranteed consistent with the spectral albedos the solver sees.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.broadband_emissivity — Function
broadband_emissivity(s, lw_spec = longwave_bands(); T_ref = 288) -> ε

Planck-weighted broadband longwave emissivity of a surface — the counterpart of broadband_albedo, and derived the same way rather than tabulated, so the scalar and the per-band values cannot disagree.

Weighted by the Planck function at T_ref, because emissivity is a property of the surface but the broadband number depends on which part of the spectrum the surface is actually radiating into. 288 K is the usual terrestrial reference.

This matters most for metal roofs. Their emissivity is ~0.25 against ≥0.90 for every other urban material, and a grey treatment that averages it away loses the reason metal roofs reach extreme daytime temperatures: they absorb like everything else but cannot radiate.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.cook_torrance_preset — Method
cook_torrance_preset(name) -> CookTorranceBRDF

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.cox_munk_slope_variance — Method
cox_munk_slope_variance(U10) -> σ²

Total mean-square wave slope as a function of the 10 m wind speed (m s⁻¹), from the sun-glitter photographs of Cox & Munk (1954):

\[σ^2 = σ_c^2 + σ_u^2 = 0.003 + 0.00512\,U_{10}\]

σ_c² (crosswind) and σ_u² (upwind) differ by about 30% in the original fit; the isotropic total is used here, split equally between the two horizontal components, because the anisotropy only matters for the azimuthal structure of the glitter pattern and not for the hemispherically integrated albedo.

The non-zero intercept at U10 = 0 is real: even a nominally calm surface carries capillary ripples from swell and surface currents.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.fresnel_polarized_reflectance — Method
fresnel_polarized_reflectance(θ, n) -> (R_s, R_p, cosθ_t)

Fresnel power reflectances of the two polarisation components at a smooth interface from air (index 1) into a non-absorbing dielectric of real refractive index n > 1, at incidence angle θ (radians):

\[r_s = \frac{\cosθ - n\cosθ_t}{\cosθ + n\cosθ_t}, \qquad r_p = \frac{n\cosθ - \cosθ_t}{n\cosθ + \cosθ_t}, \qquad R_s = r_s^2,\; R_p = r_p^2,\]

with sin θ_t = sin θ / n from Snell's law; cosθ_t (the cosine of the refraction angle inside the medium) is returned as well because the path length through an absorbing layer depends on it. By reciprocity the same R_s, R_p apply to the ray leaving the medium at θ_t. This is the single implementation of the Fresnel equations in the package: fresnel_reflectance (water) and the glazing optics of UrbanSurface both call it.

Limits: R_s = R_p = ((n-1)/(n+1))² at normal incidence, R_p = 0 at the Brewster angle atan(n), and both tend to 1 at grazing incidence.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.fresnel_reflectance — Function
fresnel_reflectance(θ, n = 1.34) -> R

Unpolarised Fresnel reflectance of a smooth dielectric interface at incidence angle θ (radians) for real refractive index n, as the mean of the two polarisation components of fresnel_polarized_reflectance:

\[R = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(R_s + R_p)\]

n = 1.34 is the appropriate broadband value for sea water across the solar spectrum (it runs from 1.35 in the blue to 1.32 at 2.5 μm; the variation changes the normal-incidence reflectance by less than 0.002 and is neglected here).

Limits: R(0) = ((n-1)/(n+1))² = 0.0211, and R → 1 as θ → π/2. The r_p component passes through zero at the Brewster angle θ_B = atan(n) = 53.3°, which is why reflected skylight off water is strongly polarised and why a polarising filter darkens it.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.hemisphere_quadrature_albedo — Method
hemisphere_quadrature_albedo(model, θ_i; nμ, nφ) -> α

Directional-hemispherical reflectance of model at incidence θ_i, by explicit nμ × nφ quadrature over the view hemisphere. This is the generic path, valid for any AbstractBRDF; black_sky_albedo calls it unless the model provides a better-conditioned specialisation.

Kept public so that a specialised method can be cross-checked against the brute-force integral, which is how the Cook-Torrance implementation below is validated.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.li_sparse_kernel — Method
li_sparse_kernel(θi, θv, Δφ; br = 1, hb = 2) -> K_geo

Li-sparse-reciprocal geometric-optical kernel (Li & Strahler 1992; Wanner et al. 1995; Lucht, Schaaf & Strahler 2000):

\[K_{geo} = O(θ_i', θ_v', Δφ) - \sec θ_i' - \sec θ_v' + \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 + \cos ξ'\bigr) \sec θ_i' \sec θ_v'\]

with the mutual-shadowing overlap area

\[O = \frac{1}{π}\,(t - \sin t \cos t)\, (\sec θ_i' + \sec θ_v'), \qquad \cos t = \frac{h}{b}\, \frac{\sqrt{D^2 + (\tanθ_i'\tanθ_v'\sin Δφ)^2}} {\sec θ_i' + \sec θ_v'},\]

\[D = \sqrt{\tan^2θ_i' + \tan^2θ_v' - 2\tanθ_i'\tanθ_v'\cosΔφ}\]

and tan θ' = (b/r) tan θ. The model idealises the scene as spheroids of vertical radius b and horizontal radius r whose centres sit at height h, casting shadows on a Lambertian background; O is the area in which the illuminated and viewed shadow ellipses overlap, which is exactly the shadow-hiding that produces the hot spot. MODIS fixes the crown shape at b/r = 1 (spherical crowns) and h/b = 2, so the kernel has no free parameters and the whole angular model is linear in the three weights.

Two details that are routinely got wrong and are handled here:

  • cos t must be clamped to [-1, 1]. Outside that range the ellipses do not overlap at all and the correct value is O = 0; a naive acos returns NaN and silently poisons the retrieval.
  • The last term carries sec θ_i' sec θ_v' (both directions), not sec θ_v' alone. That product is what makes this the reciprocal variant: swapping illumination and view leaves K_geo unchanged, as it must. The non-reciprocal variant is still occasionally published and is wrong for this purpose.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.ross_thick_kernel — Method
ross_thick_kernel(θi, θv, Δφ) -> K_vol

Ross-thick volume-scattering kernel (Roujean et al. 1992; Wanner et al. 1995):

\[K_{vol} = \frac{(π/2 - g)\cos g + \sin g}{\cos θ_i + \cos θ_v} - \frac{π}{4}\]

where g is the phase angle. It is the single-scattering radiative transfer solution for a dense, optically thick layer of randomly oriented, equally transmitting and reflecting facets — a leaf canopy with a large leaf-area index. It is normalised so that it vanishes for nadir view and nadir illumination, which makes the three-kernel model's f_iso directly interpretable as the nadir-nadir reflectance.

K_vol peaks at backscatter, so it carries part of the hot spot, but its peak is broad: the sharp part of a real hot spot comes from the geometric kernel.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.rossli_preset — Method
rossli_preset(name; kwargs...) -> RossLiBRDF

Representative broadband Ross-Li kernel weights for :grass, :forest, :soil, :sand, :snow, :urban. Note that f_geo is largest for the urban preset: a city is the archetypal sparse scene of tall objects casting long shadows on a bright background, which is exactly what the Li-sparse kernel describes.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.rpv_preset — Method
rpv_preset(name) -> RPVBRDF

Representative RPV parameters. name is one of :grass, :forest, :soil, :sand, :snow, :urban.

The trend across the presets is the physical story: dark, strongly structured canopies have small ρ0, small k (pronounced bowl shape) and strongly negative Θ (shadow hiding); bright, smooth, weakly structured surfaces move towards k → 1 and Θ → 0, and snow crosses into mild forward scattering.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.snow_grain_radius — Method
snow_grain_radius(r0, age_days; growth_rate = SNOW_GROWTH_RATE) -> μm

Effective grain radius after age_days of metamorphism, from parabolic growth r² = r₀² + G t. With the default rate, 50 μm fresh snow reaches 112 μm after a day, 230 μm after five days and 550 μm after a month.

The growth rate is strongly temperature- and gradient-dependent in reality (it roughly doubles per 10 K near the melting point, and melt metamorphism is far faster still); growth_rate is exposed so a land model that tracks snow temperature can supply its own.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.water_albedo_diffuse — Method
water_albedo_diffuse(w::FresnelWater; nμ = 40) -> α

Bihemispherical (white-sky) specular albedo of the water surface, 2∫₀¹ α_F(μ₀) μ₀ dμ₀. Close to 0.066 for flat water — three times the overhead-sun value, because an isotropic sky puts a lot of its energy at large zenith angles where the Fresnel reflectance is high. This is the number to use for the diffuse component of the incoming shortwave, and using the overhead-sun value instead is a standard and easily avoided error.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.water_albedo_direct — Method
water_albedo_direct(w::FresnelWater, θ_0; nslope = 40) -> α

Specular (Fresnel) albedo of the wind-roughened surface for a direct beam at solar zenith angle θ_0, by Gauss-Legendre integration over the two-dimensional slope distribution out to ±6σ per component. Excludes whitecaps and the water-leaving term, which surface_albedo adds.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.white_sky_albedo — Method
white_sky_albedo(model; nμi = 40, kwargs...) -> α

White-sky (bihemispherical) albedo: the reflectance of the surface under perfectly isotropic diffuse illumination,

\[α_{ws} = 2\int_0^1 α_{bs}(μ_i)\,μ_i\,dμ_i\]

This is a genuine double integral over both hemispheres — the outer quadrature here, the inner one inside black_sky_albedo. It is the albedo that applies under an overcast sky, and it is the quantity that MODIS reports as the sky-independent half of MCD43.

Aeolus.SurfaceOptics.whitecap_fraction — Method
whitecap_fraction(U10) -> W

Fraction of the sea surface covered by breaking-wave foam (Monahan & O'Muircheartaigh 1980):

\[W = 2.95\times10^{-6}\,U_{10}^{3.52}\]

Negligible below 6 m/s (< 0.2%) and a few percent in a gale. Whitecaps are bright — a broadband reflectance near 0.22 — so they matter for the ocean albedo only when the wind is strong, but there they partly offset the roughness-driven reduction of the specular term.
